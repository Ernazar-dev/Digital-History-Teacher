# The Karakalpaks in the Late Middle Ages

> The lecture was prepared based on Topic 8 of the textbook "History of Uzbekistan" by B. J. Eshov and A. A. Odilov (Book II, 2020), as well as sources related to the history of the Karakalpak people.

## 1. Sources and Historiography

Information on the history of the Karakalpaks from the 16th to the first half of the 19th century has been preserved in various types of sources. They can be divided into three groups:

Oral folklore: Folk epics (destans) such as "Alpomish," "Qirq Qiz," "Qoblan," "Mast Podsho," "Edigey," "Shahriyor," "Er Shora," "Omongeldi," and "Davlatyorbek" reflect the people's past, heroes, and customs. While an epic is valuable as a historical source, one must not overlook that its events are subject to artistic interpretation.

Written historical works: Information about the Karakalpaks is found in works such as Abulgazi Bahodir Khan's "Shajarayi Turk," Muhammad Yusuf Munshi's "Tarikh-i Muqimkhani," and Muhammad Rizo's "Firdaws ul-Iqbal." Because these works were predominantly composed at royal courts, they present events from the perspective of the rulers.

Works of Karakalpak authors: The works of Berdaq (Berdymurat Qarg'aboy ogli, 1827-1900), an outstanding figure in Karakalpak literature, particularly his genealogical work "Shejire," provide primary information on the history of the people. For this reason, Berdaq is recognized as the first Karakalpak historian. The records of Russian merchants and envoys (for instance, the Pazukhin embassy of 1669 and the trade caravan of 1693) also serve as important sources for studying the Karakalpaks' relations with neighboring states.

The topic has been investigated by scholars such as S. P. Tolstov, Ya. G. Gulyamov, P. P. Ivanov, L. S. Tolstova, B. V. Andrianov, T. A. Zhdanko, S. K. Kamalov, A. Qoshchanov, O. Jalilov, R. Kosbergenov, Kh. Yesbergenov, U. A. Abdurasulov, and M. Karlibaev. Given the diversity of the sources, a historian must cross-examine them while taking into account the purpose behind each composition and the author's viewpoint.

## 2. Origin of the Karakalpak People

Researchers consider the Saka Tigrakhauda ("Sakas with pointed hats"), mentioned in the 6th-century BCE Behistun Inscription, to be among the ancestors of the modern Karakalpaks. According to sources, the Pecheneg (Bijanak) and Oghuz ethnic groups took shape around the Aral Sea during the 6th-8th centuries CE. Research indicates that the Pecheneg tribes are considered the forebears of the Karakalpaks. The ethnogenesis of the Karakalpak people began in the 8th-10th centuries. At the beginning of the 10th century, under pressure from the Oghuz and Kipchaks, a portion of the Pechenegs was forced to migrate to southern Rus lands. As a result of the Mongol invasion, part of the Aral-region Karakalpaks relocated along the Volga and Ural rivers, as well as to the upper reaches of the Syr Darya.

In medieval Russian sources, the Karakalpaks are mentioned as "chornye klobuki" ("black hats"), while local sources refer to them as "qora borkli." The name of the people itself is linked to this traditional headwear. The name "Karakalpak" was officially recorded in 1598 in a yarlik (decree) granted by Abdullah Khan II to the sacred mausoleum of Ziyoviddin in Sighnaq. Thus, the formation of the Karakalpak people was a centuries-long, complex ethnic process involving ancient Saka tribes, medieval Turkic tribes, and later nomadic groups.

## 3. Political History in the 16th-18th Centuries

### Nomadic Life and Neighbors

In the 16th and 17th centuries, the Karakalpaks led a nomadic way of life across a vast territory. The area of their densest concentration was the middle and lower Syr Darya basin. According to Berdaq, a small number also lived around Urgench and Oqqal'a. Some sources note that in the 16th century, certain groups of Karakalpaks also settled between the Volga, Ural, and Emba rivers, as well as in the domains of the Bukhara Khanate. In the 17th century, when the Nogais began migrating to Crimea, the Karakalpaks settled around the Aral Sea and along the Syr Darya.

The Karakalpaks maintained trade and economic ties with the Ashtarkhanids of Bukhara and the Tashkent oasis. Between 1578 and 1582, they directly took part in the political life of the Bukhara Khanate; according to Russian sources, the Karakalpaks were subject to the Bukhara state. Their involvement in the political affairs of the Khiva Khanate during the 17th century is noted in Abulgazi Bahodir Khan's "Shajarayi Turk."

### Subjugation to the Kazakh Khans

Until the mid-18th century, the Karakalpaks lived along the middle and lower reaches of the Syr Darya adjacent to the Kazakhs of the Junior Zhuz, remaining economically and politically subordinate to the Kazakh khans. During the reign of the Kazakh ruler Tauke Khan (1680-1718), the Karakalpaks were governed by one of his sons: Taburchak Sultan from 1694, and his son Gaib Sultan from 1709. From 1722, Eshmuhammad Khan became the khan of the Karakalpaks.

The Karakalpaks were required to annually provide Kazakh khans with a large amount of grain and cavalry, participate in Kazakh military campaigns, and collect tribute for Kazakh sultans from caravans passing through their territory.

### Trade mediation and relations with Russia

The lower and middle reaches of the Syr Darya, where the Karakalpaks lived, were located on the trade routes leading to Tashkent, Kazan, Moscow, and China. Therefore, Karakalpak merchants acted as intermediaries between the sedentary and nomadic populations. Their trade skills were also demonstrated by a trade caravan consisting of a thousand camels sent to Russia in 1722. In the first quarter of the 18th century, relations with Bukhara and Khiva intensified, and relations with Russia were established through the Bashkirs. A 1721 source notes that Karakalpaks and Bashkirs lived with each other for one month or three weeks each year.

### The Dzungar invasion and the division of the people

In 1723, the Dzungars invaded the lands of the Kazakh Khanate. The majority of the Kazakhs, who could not resist, scattered, and the Karakalpaks were forced to split into two camps. One group moved toward the upper reaches of the Syr Darya-the Tashkent oasis-and became subordinate to the Dzungars ("upper Karakalpaks"). The second group settled in the lower reaches of the Syr Darya, along the Aral Sea, and became subject to the Kazakhs of the Lesser Zhuz ("lower Karakalpaks"). The Dzungar threat intensified the desire for unification among the Karakalpaks in various regions: ties were established between the Karakalpaks of the Syr Darya and the Karakalpaks of the Zarafshan oasis and Khorezm.

### Abulkhair Khan's attack and the migration to the Janydarya

In the 30s and 40s of the 18th century, the Karakalpaks fell under the rule of the Khan of the Junior Juz, Abulkhair. In 1743, Karakalpak ambassadors led by Mamanbiy received a "patronage" certificate from the Russian Empress Elizabeth in Saint Petersburg. Infuriated by this, Abulkhair Khan attacked the Karakalpaks of the Aral Sea region that year and plundered them. As a result, the main part of the Karakalpaks on the banks of the Syr Darya migrated toward the Janydarya. In the winter of 1746–1747, they were also attacked by the ruler of the Middle Juz, Ablai Sultan.

The main part of the Karakalpaks, who could not withstand the attacks of Kazakh khans and sultans, settled in the southwest-at the borders of the Khiva Khanate, between the Kuvandarya and Dzhanidarya rivers. In 1762, Sultan Erali forced the Karakalpaks remaining on the banks of the Syr Darya to migrate to the banks of the Janydarya and Aral. In the 18th and early 19th centuries, the Kuvandarya and Jonidarya basins were entirely the property of the Karakalpaks. Here, they formed a political alliance with the Uzbeks of the Aral Sea region and intermingled ethnically.

## 4. Karakalpaks and the Khiva Khanate.

### Muhammad Amin's policy of friendship

From the second half of the 18th century, the struggle between the Karakalpaks and the rulers of Khiva intensified. Muhammad Amin-inak (1763–1790) strengthened central power and attempted to subjugate the Karakalpaks to the khanate. He won over influential clan leaders to his side: his authority was recognized by the prominent Karakalpak biys Esongeldi mahram and Oydustbiy. According to the historian Munis, Oydustbiy "later attained the rank of ruler of the entire Karakalpak Ulus." Muhammad Amin inoq granted land to the Karakalpak nobility. During this period, the development of lands along the Aral and Amu Darya rivers and the construction of water facilities began. For example, the leader of the Kenagas tribe, Amankulibi, dug a large canal from the Amu Darya and improved the water supply for his tribe.

### Subjugation under Muhammad Rahim Khan I

Tura Murad Sufi and Khoja Murad Sufi, who separated from the khanate and established the Aral Sea Governorate, also relied on the Karakalpak clan leaders. During the reign of Muhammad Rahim Khan I, a policy of total subjugation of the Karakalpaks began. In January 1809, the population around the Kazakh Darya and the Aral Sea, including the Karakalpaks, was subjugated; the Khan allowed and assisted Oydustbiy in building a fortress bearing his name, which later became an important fortification in the occupation of the Aral Sea region. In 1810, the Karakalpaks on the right bank of the Amu Darya-Shurkul and Jonidarya-were subjugated. In 1811, four campaigns were launched against Kungrad, the administration of the Aral Sea region was abolished, Kungrad was occupied, and the Karakalpaks of Kungrad and the Aral Sea region fully recognized the rule of the Khiva Khanate. The Khan ordered certain Karakalpak tribes to migrate to these lands to develop the lands of northern Khwarazm.

### Protests and riots

During the reign of Allaqulikhan (1825–1842), most of the privileges granted to Oydustbiy were abolished. The robbery and abuse of power by the Khan's deputy, Gadoi Niyaz Mahram, caused strong discontent among the Karakalpaks. Between 1855 and 1856, another uprising against the khanate took place. Despite this, the Karakalpak population actively participated in the economic life and trade of the khanate. In the mid-19th century, the administrative center of the Karakalpaks moved to the city of Chimbay; the governor of the Khiva Khan also settled here.

### 1873 and after

According to the Treaty of Gandimiyan concluded with the Russian Empire in 1873, the Khanate of Khiva became a vassal of Russia. The territories of the Karakalpaks on the right bank of the Amu Darya were annexed to Russia, and the Amu Darya Department was established; From 1877, it became part of the Syrdarya region of the Turkestan Governor-Generalship. The small number of Karakalpaks on the left bank remained dependent on the Khiva Khanate until 1920.

## 5. Social structure and governance

### Social stratification

In the 19th century, tribal traditions were preserved within the social system of the Karakalpaks. On one side stood the wealthy, the owners of large plots of land and livestock, and on the other, the poor citizens who worked for them. Although the khan granted lands to the Karakalpaks of Khwarazm for the use of tribes and clans, the best lands belonged to the heads of the tribes and clans.

Three types of land ownership in the Khiva Khanate-state (khan) lands, property lands, and waqf lands-were also preserved on the territory of the Karakalpaks. Large landowners included biys, atalyks, beglarbegis, rich men, merchants, judges, rais, mirabs, batyrs, as well as mullahs, ishans, and akhuns. The land of the peasants did not exceed one or two tanobs; small or landless peasants farmed on other people's lands in exchange for a part of the harvest or on the condition of labor; most of them went to the south of Khorezm to work on the lands of the Uzbek and Turkmen rich. The best pastures, lakes, and reed beds belonged to the nobility; officials and priests owned thousands of head of livestock.

### Control system

In the 18th century, the Karakalpaks were governed by the advice of biys. Each tribal union was headed by a biy, who owned large land and livestock. The "fourteen clans"-a union of several tribes and clans-were ruled by the biys of the Manghit clan. Until the second half of the 19th century, the main positions were biy (tribal chief), juzbashi (hundred-man), mahram (zakot collector), mirob, qazi, and rais. The main one was the biy, who collected taxes into the khan's treasury and resolved internal tribal disputes-land, water, and criminal cases.

From the second half of the 19th century, the rights of biys were restricted. The Khan of Khiva, Sayyid Muhammad Khan, introduced the additional positions of two naibs, six atalyks, two beklarbegi, and one agabi for the administration of the Karakalpaks. The primary administrative unit was the aul, which was governed by a council of elders. The main administrative centers were Chimbay on the right bank of the Amu Darya and Kungrad on the left bank.

### Taxes and obligations

After the conquest, taxes and obligations in the khanate were introduced for the Karakalpaks: product payments, an annual tax of 20,000 tenge, the maintenance of soldiers in the khan's army and the provision of nukers, and the digging and cleaning of irrigation ditches and canals (hashar). Sources note that these obligations were a heavy burden for the Karakalpak population.

## 6. Economic life.

### Location and agricultural oases

At the beginning of the 19th century, the settlement of the Karakalpaks in Khorezm, on both banks of the Amu Darya, was largely completed: on the left bank between Kungrad and Khojeyli, in the basin of the Shumanay canal; on the right bank along the Kokouzak and Kechitli canals and the banks of the Amu Darya. Creating an irrigation system to transform the steppe into fertile land on new lands required great labor and time. The main types of farming were agriculture, animal husbandry, and fishing.

In the first quarter of the 19th century, three agricultural oases were formed: Kallikol (which received water from the Lavdin, Shumanay, and Kiyat-Jargan canals), Kushkannatov (to the northwest of Chimbay, which received water from the Karabeyli tributary), and Kegeyli. In the mid-19th century, the Chimbay and Kungrad economic districts were formed. Agriculture was entirely dependent on the water of the Amu Darya, and there was a constant risk of flooding and drought. The land was tilled with a plow and a harrow driven by an ox. Wheat, rice, barley, sorghum, millet, sesame, oats, melons, watermelons, pumpkins, onions, carrots, mung beans, and peas were grown. Three varieties of rice-purple, white, and black-were in high demand in both domestic and foreign markets. Cotton is mainly grown around Kungrad. In horticulture, oleaster, apricots, peaches, and apples were grown; one traveler noted orchards stretching three to five versts along the irrigation ditches around Chimbay.

### Livestock, fish, and handicrafts

The Aral Sea region, the Kyzylkum, and the tributaries of the Amu Darya were favorable for livestock breeding; cattle, sheep, and goats were primarily raised, and camels were kept only by the wealthy. Fish farming is developed in the Aral, Amu Darya, Davkara, Kungrad, and Kara Tereng lakes, and hunting is practiced during the autumn-winter months. There were types of craftsmanship such as blacksmithing, sewing, weaving, handicrafts, pottery, jewelry, and carpentry. Products were produced primarily for domestic consumption; craftsmen received grain and livestock for their labor.

### Trade

Caravan routes passing through the north of the Khiva Khanate connected Chimbay, Khojeyli, Kungrad, and Old Urgench with Khiva; The road connecting Khiva with Kazalinsk passed through Chimbay, and the road from Orenburg and Uralsk through Ustyurt passed near Kungrad. From the 19th century, the Karakalpaks began to take an active part in the khanate's trade: they sold wheat, livestock, and fish at the bazaars and purchased Russian, Khivan, and Bukharan goods. Uzbek and Turkmen merchants bought fish and cattle from them, while Kazakhs bought grain. There were many shops and caravanserais around Chimbay.

## 7. Cultural life.

In the late Middle Ages, Karakalpak culture developed in a unique way, despite difficult natural conditions and dependence on the Khivan khans. Cities and fortresses such as Kungrad, Kiyat, Chimbay, Nukus, Kipchak, Mangit, Janga-kala, Oydust-kala, Kukouzak, and Eshon-kala were centers of cultural and architectural monuments.

### Education

Every village had a school and a mullah who taught there; the duties of a teacher were also performed by the imam of a mosque, a muazzin, or an educated person. Boys aged 5–6 were admitted to the schools, where they learned to read and write and recite the Quran and hadiths. If the school was located at a mosque, the teacher's salary was paid from waqf income. Graduates of the school studied in the madrasas of Khiva and Bukhara, as well as in local madrasas. In the mid-19th century, 58 madrasas operated on the right bank of modern Karakalpakstan; among them, the Karakum Ishan and the Tash Madrasah (Tas Madrasah) held great prestige. Education in madrasas was two-stage: the first taught Arabic grammar, and the second taught fiqh, logic, history, and other sciences.

### Literature & Music

Among the Karakalpaks, zhyrau-bakhshi were well-developed: Zhiyen zhyrau (1730–1784), Shankay zhyrau (1814–1884), and Zhismurat zhyrau (1836–1908) were famous. The 19th-century poets called Jiyen Zhyrau the "father of Karakalpak poetry." Kunkhoja Ibrahim ugli was a poet who lived in poverty, and his poems "Reapers," "White Reed," and "Shepherds" are famous. The ideas of patriotism and humanism prevail in the poems of Ajiniyaz Kosibay ugli (1811-1878); The epic "Buzatau" celebrates the forced resettlement of the people. Berdakh (1827-1900) was a prominent representative of Karakalpak classical literature, whose poem "For the People" was a call for action for his time; The works "Shajara," "Oydustbiy," "Ernazarbiy," and "Omangeldi" reflect historical processes. Atash Olshinbay ugli (1828–1902) turned to historical events, and his works "Gulziba" and "Girls" depicted the lives of women.

The core of Karakalpak music consists of folk songs: wedding yalla (khajar, betashar), lullabies (besik zhiri), and "healing" melodies (badik). The main instruments were the kobyz, dutar, changkovuz, surnay, and gijjak.

### Religious and domestic life

In the 17th and first half of the 19th centuries, the spiritual life of the Karakalpaks was linked to the Yasawiyya and Naqshbandi orders. The authority of Imam Ishan, whose residence was in Kirantau, was great, and this territory became the religious and cultural center of the Karakalpaks on the right bank of the Amu Darya. The tombs of Akbilakoyim, Rustambabo, Imam Eshon, and Bektemir Eshon, as well as places of worship such as Sultan Uvays, Narindjan Baba, Shamun Nabi, and Hakim Ota, were popular pilgrimage sites. Changes also occurred in everyday life: four different styles of housing construction appeared, yurts (tents) and courtyards were organized in a new way, and their decoration with patterns and ornaments rose to the level of art. Men's and women's clothing and jewelry were also decorated with unique patterns.

## 8. Specific features and historical significance of the history of the Karakalpak people.

### The complexity of political fate

The main feature of the history of the Karakalpaks is that for a long time, this people did not have their own independent state, but lived in a territory where the influence of several powerful neighboring states — the Kazakh Khanate, the Dzungar Khanate, the Bukhara and Khiva khanates, and the Russian Empire — intersected. These conditions forced the people to constantly change their political course and maintain a balance between various forces. The Dzungar invasion of 1723 and the subsequent migrations brought part of the people to the Tashkent oasis and another part to the Aral Sea region, making it difficult for them to unite into a single political organization. Nevertheless, the diplomatic activities of the Karakalpak biys, including attempts to establish relations with Russia (Mamanbiy's embassy in 1743), demonstrate the people's desire to independently decide their own destiny.

### Relations with neighboring peoples

Close ties with the Uzbek, Kazakh, and Turkmen peoples played an important role in the formation of the Karakalpak people. Especially in the Kuvandarya and Jonidarya basins, the Karakalpaks formed a political alliance with the Uzbeks of the Aral Sea region and intermingled ethnically. Living side by side with Uzbeks in the Khorezm oasis led to the exchange of experience in irrigated agriculture, craftsmanship, and trade, as well as cultural interaction. As part of the Khiva Khanate, the Karakalpaks actively participated in economic life, which accelerated the process of their transformation from nomadic pastoralists into settled peasants and merchants.

### Transition from nomadism to settlement

The most important change in Karakalpak society at the end of the 18th and the first half of the 19th centuries was the transition from a nomadic and semi-nomadic lifestyle to sedentary agriculture. This process was linked to the construction of new canals in the lower reaches of the Amu Darya, the development of steppe lands, and the creation of an irrigation system. The formation of the Kallikol, Koshkannatov, and Kegeyli oases was the result of this process. Settlement also affected the social structure: clan and tribal ties gradually weakened, and relations based on land ownership, taxes, and obligations developed. However, the influence of clan chiefs and traditional forms of government (the council of biys) persisted until the second half of the 19th century.

### Significance of cultural heritage

The rich oral creativity of the Karakalpak people, their traditions of epic and zhyrau poetry, and the works of poets such as Berdakh, Ajiniyaz, and Kunkhodzha were the primary carriers of the people's historical memory. In particular, in his work "Shajara," Berdakh described the history of the people in poetic form, harmonizing historiography and literature. When studying them as historical sources, it is important to distinguish between artistic interpretation and historical truth, and to draw conclusions by comparing several sources. Studying the history of the Karakalpak people allows for a deeper understanding of the multifaceted and complex picture of the history of Uzbekistan and Central Asia as a whole.

## Conclusion

In the late Middle Ages, the Karakalpak people passed through a complex historical path: from nomadism along the Syr Darya, from splits and migrations caused by the invasion of Kazakh khans and Dzungars, to sedentary farming in the Amu Darya basin. At the beginning of the 19th century, the Karakalpaks, subjected to the Khiva Khanate, built irrigation systems, agricultural oases, and trade centers despite heavy taxes and obligations. Their rich oral creativity, the tradition of the zhyrau, and the works of poets such as Berdakh and Ajiniyaz became an important part of the people's spirituality.

## Key concepts

- Sakas with peaked caps (Tigrakhauda) are considered one of the ancient ancestors of the Karakalpaks.
- **Bijanak, Oghuz** — ethnic groups formed in the Aral Sea region.
- "Chyornye klobuki," "kara borkli" are the names of the Karakalpaks in Russian and local sources.
- **Upper and Lower Karakalpaks** — two groups after 1723.
- **Biy, juzbasy, mahram, aul** — clan chief, centurion, zakat collector and primary administrative unit.
- **Solgut-kesma, hashar** — an annual tax and the obligation to dig and clean ditches.
- Zhirov is a Karakalpak folk poet and dastanist.
