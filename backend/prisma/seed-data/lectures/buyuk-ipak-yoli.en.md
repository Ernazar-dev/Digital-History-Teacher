# The Great Silk Road is a path of dialogue and cooperation

**Lecture objective:** to provide students with a holistic understanding of the emergence of ancient communication routes in Central Asia, the formation, directions, and stages of development of the Great Silk Road, its role in the dialogue of civilizations, and its modern significance; to teach them to analyze archaeological finds as historical evidence.

## 1. Ancient communication routes

The emergence and development of ancient trade routes in Central Asia have undergone several historical stages. The first communication routes began to form during the Stone Age. The most ancient roads connected settlements with food and water sources, raw material reserves, and had a narrow local significance. Two processes played an important role in the formation of the first roads. Differences in the lifestyle and economy of the ancient population, as well as the uneven distribution of raw material reserves, led to exchange between neighboring tribes, and from the late Mesolithic and Neolithic periods, between distant territories. Secondly, these migrations and the seizure of territories rich in water and raw materials, convenient for life, contributed to the emergence of new roads connecting remote areas.

### Eneolithic and Bronze Age

In the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages, farms specializing in irrigated agriculture and animal husbandry emerged. Ancient farmers gradually mastered river valleys from south to north, laying the foundation for early urbanization and urban planning culture. Cattle-breeding tribes, occupying mountains and foothills, and vast steppes, laid the foundation for nomadic livestock breeding during the Late Bronze and Early Iron Ages. The development of metallurgy also had a noticeable impact on the expansion of the road network.

During this period, two groups of roads were formed. Roads of local significance connected settled agricultural centers with sown areas, pastures, hunting grounds, raw material deposits, and water sources, ensuring the movement of livestock breeders to pastures and for the winter. Regional roads connected various cultures of Central Asia: for example, roads connecting the settlements of Anov, Namozgoh, Altyntepa (Southern Turkmenistan), Sapalli, Jarqutan (Surkhan oasis), Tazabagyab (Khorezm), Zamanbaba, and Sarazm (Zarafshan). From the Bronze Age, the specialization of roads for trade and cultural ties intensified: settlements for passengers, water and food supplies, and security services began to form on caravan routes.

During this period, discoveries that accelerated the development of the civilization of the Ancient East (Mesopotamia, Western Asia, Egypt, Elam, the Indus River valley, Central Asia) appeared: metallurgy, irrigated agriculture, city-states, water transport, horses, camels, donkeys, and carts. Therefore, the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages are considered the period when international relations between Central Asia and the Ancient East were first established.

## 2. Connections between the Blue Way and the Bronze Age

### Blue Way

An important role in the emergence of transit routes connecting Central Asia with the centers of the Ancient East was played by the economic factor—the high demand for metals, precious stones, and minerals extracted in the Ancient East from the south of Central Asia. Among them was Badakhshan lapis lazuli, which was highly valued in the Ancient East. The Blue Route is the oldest transcontinental route of external relations in Central Asia. It began with the azure mines in Gorno-Badakhshan and reached Mesopotamia and Egypt. The road from the mines passed through Shurtukay on the left bank of the Amu Darya and branched off here: one direction went to Northern India through the valley of the Kabuldarya and the passes of the Hindu Kush; The Mesopotamian route went to Mesopotamia through Mundigak (near Kandahar) and Shahri Sokhta in Iranian Seistan, through settlements on the coast of the Persian Gulf.

Products made of Badakhshan lapis lazuli were found in the ruins of the city of Uruk in Mesopotamia, in the "King's Tomb" in the city of Ur. In Sumerian ceramic inscriptions, it is noted that gold, silver, and lapis lazuli were brought from distant mountainous areas. The branches of this road are also evidenced by azure objects found in Troy in Asia Minor and in the fortress of Maykop in the Caucasus. These data indicate that the deposits of Badakhshan were exploited in the 7th century BC. It indicates the beginning of the 4th millennium BC.

### Relations with Iran, Mesopotamia, and India

During the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages, a system of roads was formed that connected the southern and central regions of Central Asia with Iran and Mesopotamia. Its main route passed through Anov, Namozgoh, and Altyntepa in Southern Turkmenistan. In the Sarazm settlement in the upper reaches of Zarafshon, seals and other finds corresponding to Mesopotamian traditions are encountered; the road ran in the direction of Mesopotamia — Shahri Sukhta — Namozgoh — Zamanbaba — Sarazm.

BC. From the 4th–3rd millennia BC, close ties were established between Central Asia and India. Permanent contacts were established with the Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro cultures in the Indus River valley; an intermediate point was the Quetta monument in Northern Baluchistan. BC. From the 2nd millennium BC, roads connecting India with the Murghab oasis were formed. The transit route from India through the Surkhan oasis was divided into several branches: through the Iron Gates into the Kashkadarya oasis, then into the middle and lower reaches of the Sarazm and Zarafshon rivers, and into the Central Kyzylkum; Along the Amu Darya to the Khorezm oasis; Through Dashli to the Murghab Valley and Southern Turkmenistan. In the Bronze Age, a road also began to function from India through Central Asia to the Urals and Siberia: Indian jewelry was transported to the north, and metal raw materials to the south.

### Early contacts with China

The textbook emphasizes an important point: Long before the Great Silk Road began its activities, back in the Bronze Age, ties between Central Asia and China were established. Silk products were found at the Bronze Age site of Sopallitepa in the Surkhan oasis. During the Late Bronze – Early Iron Age, an iron sickle, a bronze mirror, and ceramic vessels similar to those of the Chust culture brought from Central Asia were found at the Kuanbak site on the southern slopes of the Tien Shan. This testifies to the ancient connections between the Choch oasis and the Fergana Valley and Eastern Turkestan.

Thus, in the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages, there were roads connecting Central Asia with India, Iran, Mesopotamia, Western Asia, Egypt, Eastern Turkestan, and China, as well as with Siberia and the Trans-Ural region through the northern steppes. In them, economic factors held a leading position, and these ties exerted a great influence on the emergence of Central Asian civilization centers.

## 3. BC. Millennium contacts

BC. 1st millennium BC The first half of the 1st millennium BC was a period of high development for the internal and external relations of Central Asia. Large trading cities, ancient state associations, and historical-cultural regions—Parthia, Bactria, Sogdiana, Margiana, Khwarazm, Fergana, Shash, and Ustrushana—were formed, and the caravan routes between them acquired stable directions. In the Early Iron Age and the Achaemenid period, political interests played an important role in foreign relations alongside economic factors.

During this period, three distinct directions are identified. The steppe road led from the Altai to Iran, Mesopotamia, Arabia, and North Africa, and from the steppes between the Itil (Volga) and the Ural to India. The gold road began with gold deposits in the Altai Mountains and went to India and Iran. The Shah's road connected the central cities of the Achaemenid Empire, Persepolis and Suz, with the satrapies: the central road in the direction of Bactria – Marv – Nis – Ekbatan – Persepolis was joined in Marv by roads from Sogdia (Marakand, Nautaka, Bukhara, Paikend) and Khwarazm.

## 4. The Formation and Directions of the Great Silk Road

### Name and start

According to researchers, a clear trade and transit route of international significance emerged in the 4th century BC. It began to form in the 2nd–1st centuries BC. The name "Silk Road" was first used in 1877 by the German scientist Ferdinand von Rechthofen; it was later recognized worldwide as the "Great Silk Road."

During the reign of the Han Empire, Emperor Wu-di (c. 140-86 BC) sought an ally against the Hun. In 138, diplomat Zhang Jian set out for Central Asia. Zhang Jian was held captive by the Xiongnu for several years. In 128–126, he reached Fergana (Davana). Although a military alliance was not established, its mission was of great importance to China in its expansion into western lands. BC. In 111-105 BC. U-di sent ambassadors to Parthia (Ansi in Chinese sources), Kangju, and Bactria, establishing diplomatic and trade relations. Thus, B.C. In the 2nd–1st centuries BC, the Great Silk Road was laid, connecting East and West. Its first branch began in the city of Xi'an in China and stretched through Eastern Turkestan, Central Asia, Iran, and Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean Sea.

### Main industries

The Great Silk Road consisted of several main branches. The southern branch (Nandao) ran in the direction of Yumenguan-Shanshan-Lobnor Lake-northern ranges of Kunlun-Yarkand-Vahan Pass-Badakhshan, from where the road branched off to Bactria and Parthia, as well as to India. The central branch passed through Uzgen – Osh – Kuva – Margilan – Kokand – Khujand – Samarkand – Bukhara and was divided from Bukhara into two parts: the southern direction – Bukhara – Karshi – Guzar – Kesh – Termez – Balkh and India through the Hindukush passes, and the western direction – Bukhara – Marv – Nishapur – Tehran – Qazvin – Hamadan – Baghdad – Palmyra – the city of Tir on the Mediterranean coast. The northern branch (Beidao) runs along the slopes of the eastern Tien Shan to Kashgar, through the pass to Fergana, then to Kangju and west. In addition to the main caravan routes, there were also internal trade routes.

### A chain of civilizations

BC. At the end of the 1st millennium BC and the beginning of the 1st millennium AD, the borders of states stretching from the Pacific Ocean to the Atlantic Ocean—China, the Khanate, the Kushan Empire, the Kangju state, Parthia, and the Roman Empire—contacted. For the first time in human history, these empires were linked by a single road. Its total length was 12,000 km. If you look at the map, you can see that Central Asia is at the center of the system of ancient civilizational ties. Thanks to this, important ethnic processes took place here, and the interaction of cultures intensified.

Along with trade along the Great Silk Road, diplomatic treaties and military alliances were concluded; The peoples of Central Asia made a huge contribution to the spread of alphabetic writing and world religions—Buddhism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and Islam—to the depths of Asia and the Far East. The textbook emphasizes that the Great Silk Road was not only a caravan route but also a process that left an indelible mark on the history of Eurasian civilization.

## 5. Infrastructure and service system of caravan routes

### Shipping &amp; Transportation

The use of horses, camels, and donkeys as means of transport began in the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages. The invention of the wheel led to the emergence of the first means of transport for large animals—the cart. Horse and wheeled transport occupied the main place from the Eneolithic-Bronze period until the emergence of modern transport.

### Road structures

An important link in the system of trade routes is the roadside structures — caravanserais, rabat and sardoba. Starting from the Late Bronze and Early Iron Ages, they served as a resting place for trade caravans, travelers, troops, and ambassadors, a source of food and fodder for pack animals, and played an important role in ensuring road safety. On the caravan routes of Central Asia, a wide system of service was established that met the requirements of the time: there were translators, conductors, guards, healers, and carters serving their animals. Central and local authorities paid great attention to road repairs and ensuring safety. Political stability was crucial for the effective functioning of this system.

### Cities and trade

In accordance with the development of international roads, the process of urbanization gradually developed: cities, fortresses, and villages appeared along the roads. Roads changed their direction for various reasons: in industries that lost their significance, cities experienced crisis, and new centers emerged along new roads. Agricultural and livestock products, high-quality handicraft products, rare metals, and precious stones occupied the primary place in trade. Central Asian products satisfied domestic needs and were sold to neighboring countries; silk and other elegant fabrics, porcelain, and metal products were brought from abroad. Products were sold through exchange, cash, and even through a checking system. The rulers supported trade by constructing roadside structures and markets, ensuring the safety of caravans, providing benefits to foreign merchants, and concluding international trade agreements. Caravan routes, along with trade, were also ways of cultural communication, the dissemination of scientific and technological innovations, and the interaction of religious and philosophical teachings.

### Waterways

In Central Asia, located far from the sea, water routes passing through the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, as well as their tributaries, occupied an important place. There were professions such as the manufacture and repair of boats and ships, as well as the transportation of goods and passengers; cities and fortresses appeared on riverbanks that controlled crossings and collected duties.

## 6. Medieval trade routes

### Early Middle Ages (V-VIII centuries)

During this period, the geography of internal and external relations expanded rapidly. Based on the Turkic Khaganate's ties with Byzantium, economic, cultural, and diplomatic relations developed along the northern branch of the Great Silk Road. The Samarkand-Nakhshab-Kesh-Iron Gate-Tokharistan road was of great importance. In the diary of the Chinese traveler and Buddhist monk Xuanzang, Chaganian and Termez are mentioned as major trade centers of the Surkhan oasis. The significance of the Samarkand-Bukhara route increased: Bukhara is mentioned in Chinese sources as "An" and connected the Khagan's residence in Semirechye with Iran via Poykand, Amul, and Marv; The envoys of the Turkic Khagan, led by the Sogdian merchant Maniakh, also used this route.

From Tokharistan and Sogdia through Khwarazm to the Caspian Sea, from Ustyurt to the Lower Volga; Central Asian coins and items found in the North Caucasus testify to ties with Byzantium and the Khazar Khaganate. The cities of the Fergana Valley held an important place in the relations between Central Asia and China. The Early Middle Ages are characterized as a period of high development in international trade, the activities of merchants, and large trade unions.

### 9th — early 13th century

This period was a developed stage of international trade. Marv played an important role in trade with Iran and Baghdad. From Baghdad, through Nishapur, Saraxs, Marw, and the Amu Darya, the road led to Bukhara; On the Marv-Amul road, there were rabatts, wells, and signal towers with lights so that caravans would not get lost at night. The main road from Iran and Khorasan to China passed through Samarkand, Shash, and Fergana. The caravan traveled from the Amudarya ford at Farab through Bukhara, Samarkand, and Khujand to Uzgan in 23 days; From Samarkand to Khujand — in 8 days. The Tashkent oasis played an important role in relations with Semirechye and Eastern Turkestan: Arab geographers name 27 cities in Shash and 14 in Ilak. The cities in the lower reaches of the Syr Darya played an important role in relations with pastoralist tribes; The city of Jend was the primary crossing point from Khwarazm to Farab.

In the 10th–12th centuries, the caravan system reached a high level of development, encompassing all major cities and villages, mines, and pastures, and a service system was established. Routes from the Mediterranean Sea via Baghdad to Iran, Maveraunnahr, and China, and from Maveraunnahr through Khwarazm to the Khazar and Bulgar kingdoms of the Volga region and Eastern Europe, were consistently utilized.

### Mongol era

Genghis Khan's conquest had a negative impact on the trade routes system: due to the destruction of trade and craft centers and the failure of irrigation structures, communication along the Great Silk Road ceased for many years. Only from the 60s–70s of the 13th century did the cities begin to be restored. A day's journey away, special stations—pits—were constructed, the costs of which were assigned to the residents along the road. The importance of the northern branch increased during this period, as steppe roads located outside the city culture were less damaged during the conquests. European ambassadors crossed the Black Sea to Saroy Berke, then crossed the steppes north of the Aral Sea, the Semirechye, and Eastern Turkestan to Karakorum. Marco Polo's "Book of the Wonders of the World" (his father's and uncle's travels in 1254-1269, as well as Marco's own travels in 1271-1295) and the work of the Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta contain important information about roads. Sources also mention the "Khan's Road," which ran from Yangikent in the lower reaches of the Syr Darya through Zarnuk to Nuruta. In general, the period of Mongol rule is assessed in the textbook as a period of crisis for caravan trade in Central Asia.

## 7. The Great Silk Road and Modernity

In 1987, within the framework of the UN World Decade for Cultural Development, UNESCO adopted the program "The Great Silk Road — the Path of Dialogue." Its goal is to deeply study the history of Central Asian civilization and to strengthen cultural and economic ties between East and West. Within the framework of the program, expeditions such as "In the Footsteps of Marco Polo" (1987), "The Sea" (1990–1991), "In the Republics of Central Asia" (1991, completed in Khiva), "In the Direction of Nomadic Cultures" (1992), international conferences in Samarkand (1990), Bukhara (1996), and other cities were conducted.

Uzbekistan also pays special attention to this issue. The International Institute for Central Asian Studies has opened in Samarkand; one of its main areas is the study of the Great Silk Road and its cities. In December 1996, the Tajan-Sarakhs railway (295 km) was opened, followed by the Mashhad-Sarakhs railway in May 1997; Central Asian countries have gained access to the Persian Gulf, and the time for exporting goods has been reduced by two weeks. In May 1998, the issue of the "Europe-Caucasus-Asia" transport corridor (TRACECA), which connects Europe and Asia, was considered in Brussels and a declaration was adopted; On September 8, 1998, at the Baku Conference, representatives from 32 countries and international organizations discussed the TRACECA program and signed a multilateral agreement on international transportation. The volume of cargo imported and exported to Uzbekistan via the TRACECA corridor amounted to nearly 140 thousand tons in 1996, and reached 285 thousand tons in 1997.

The textbook also notes the problems of reviving the traditions of the Great Silk Road: Uzbekistan has no access to the sea, although 80% of international trade is carried out by sea; the prolonged war in neighboring Afghanistan has made direct cooperation difficult. Today, the revival of the Great Silk Road is acquiring increasing global significance. According to the textbook's conclusion, this path is a path of friendship, culture, economic prosperity, and cooperation between countries—in short, a path of progress.

## Conclusion

The communication routes of Central Asia began with local stone-age paths and transitioned into transcontinental roads, such as the Blue Route, during the Bronze Age. In the 2nd–1st centuries, it transformed into the Great Silk Road, which stretched from China to the Mediterranean Sea. Archaeological finds (lazurite, silk, jade, seals) prove that written sources testify to the significant antiquity of these ties. Since Central Asia was at the center of this system, it played a decisive role in the exchange of trade, religion, writing, science, and technology. The prosperity of roads depended on political stability: if the Mongol conquest led to their decline, today they are being restored through international transport projects.

## Key concepts

- The Blue Road is the oldest trans-regional road from Badakhshan to Mesopotamia and Egypt.
- Local, regional, and trans-regional roads are types of roads based on their significance.
- **Steppe Road, Golden Road, Royal Road** — BC Directions in the 1st millennium.
- **The Great Silk Road** — BC An international road that formed in the 2nd–1st centuries and stretched from China to the Mediterranean Sea (term - F. von Richthofen, 1877).
- **Zhang Jian** — Han Dynasty diplomat sent to Western countries.
- **Caravanserai, rabat, sardoba** — roadside structures; yom - a postal station during the Mongol era.
- **TRACECA** — the "Europe - Caucasus - Asia" transport corridor
