## 1. The concept of the "Oriental Renaissance"

The 9th-12th centuries, when masters of encyclopedic knowledge lived and made unique changes in science, culture, and art, are known as the Eastern Renaissance. In terms of its features, it is compared to the Renaissance of the 15th-16th centuries in Western Europe. The term "Renaissance" was first used by the 16th-century Italian artist and historian J. Vasari; The term "Muslim Renaissance" was proposed by the European orientalist A. Mets. In relation to the East, this term is used conventionally: it refers not only to the revival of Greek-Hellenistic culture but also to the revival of ancient culture and the national values of the peoples themselves. Davr V.V. Bartold, I.Yu. Krachkovsky, Ya. Gulyamov, I. Muminov, B. Akhmedov, and many foreign scientists.

## 2. Factors of cultural growth. "Bayt ul-Hikma"

### Factors

The cultural upsurge in the 9th-12th centuries was the result of the interaction of several factors. First, the unification of various peoples within a vast caliphate contributed to the development of internal and external trade, metallurgy, textiles, paper and glass production, and artificial irrigation. Secondly, economic needs created a demand for exact and natural sciences: astronomy, geometry (structures, shipbuilding), medicine, and mineralogy acquired practical significance. Thirdly, the Arabic language became the language of science and united scholars from different nations into a single space of scientific communication. Fourth, Islam promoted the pursuit of knowledge in the Quran and hadiths. Fifth, in the 9th-10th centuries, independent states (the Samanids, Karakhanids, Ghaznavids, Seljuks, and Khwarazmshahs) were formed in Maveraunnahr, and political stability was established. Finally, the rulers gathered scholars, poets, and artists in their palaces and organized libraries. As Yaqut al-Hamawi wrote: "Mawarannahr is one of the most beautiful, fertile, and wealthy lands... And in terms of beauty, there is no place in the whole world more beautiful than Bukhara.

### "Bayt ul-Hikma"

In 762, the capital of the caliphate was moved to Baghdad. Caliph Harun al-Rashid (786-809) and especially al-Ma'mun (813-833) patronized science. During his stay in Marw, al-Ma'mun attracted Khwarazmi, Fergani, Javhari, and other scholars to his court and moved with them to Baghdad in 819. Thus, the "Khalifa Ma'mun Academy" — "Bayt ul-Hikma" ("House of Wisdom") — was established in Baghdad. There, works in Greek, Syriac, Sanskrit, Pahlavi, and Khwarazmian were translated into Arabic; In 828, observatories were built in Baghdad and in 831 near Damascus; In 830, scientific expeditions to India and Byzantium were organized under the leadership of al-Khwarizmi. As the German researcher H. Zuter notes, most of the scholars of this center were from Khorasan, Maveraunnahr, and Fergana; Later in Europe, they were mistakenly called "Arab scholars."

### Education and libraries in Maveraunnahr

Simultaneously with the scientific center in Baghdad, science developed in Maveraunnahr and Khorasan itself. Bukhara during the Samanid era became one of the prestigious scientific centers of the Islamic world: the rich library at the ruler's court was open to scholars, and Ibn Sina wrote in his autobiography that he used this library in his youth and became acquainted with many rare books. The establishment of paper production in Samarkand facilitated the writing and printing of books, which facilitated the widespread dissemination of scientific knowledge. Samarkand paper was highly valued in the East and West during the Middle Ages.

The education system consisted of school and madrasah stages, where children first learned to read and write in school and then studied religious and secular sciences in madrasas. In the 11th century, the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk opened "Nizamiya" madrasas in the cities and expanded the system of training officials and scholars. Madrasas were funded by waqf revenues, which led to a significant popularization of education. In the palaces of rulers, the creation of private libraries and the holding of scientific discussions (conferences) between scholars were widespread. As a result, scientists freely moved from one city to another and exchanged scientific experience, which allowed for the faster dissemination of knowledge throughout the region.

## 3. Encyclopaedic scholars: al-Khwarizmi, al-Fergani, Farabi

### Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (783-850)

The great mathematician, astronomer, and geographer al-Khwarizmi was born in Khwarazm and led the activities of the "Bayt ul-Hikma" in Baghdad. He is the founder of algebra: the word "algebra" is taken from his work "Al-kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisob al-jabr wa al-muqobala." His arithmetic treatise introduced Europe to Indian numerals and the decimal positional (zero-based) counting system; its Latin translation begins with the words "Dixit Algorizmi" ("Al-Khwarizmi said"), and the scientist's name is immortalized in the term "algorithm." If "Zij" (c. 830) is one of the first astronomical works in the caliphate, then "Kitab surat ul-arz" is a geographical work in which the enriched part of the earth is divided into seven climates. Of the more than 20 works, 10 have reached us. J. Sarton called him "the greatest mathematician of his time and one of the greatest of all time."

### Ahmad al-Farghani (797-865)

An astronomer, mathematician, and geographer from the village of Kuva in the Fergana Valley. His "Book of Celestial Movements and General Astronomy" was translated into Latin twice in the 12th century and became the main astronomy textbook in European universities; the Latin name is Alfraganus. The Latin translation of the work was published in 1493, making it one of the earliest printed books. In 861, on the island of Ravza near Cairo, he built a device for measuring the water level of the Nile (the nilometer). He was remembered by Regiomontanus, Dante, and Schiller; In 1998, the 1200th anniversary of his birth was celebrated.

### Abu Nasr Farabi (873-950)

The philosopher and encyclopedic scholar Farabi, who was born in Farab on the banks of the Syr Darya, studied in Shash, Bukhara, Samarkand, and Baghdad, and died in Damascus. He wrote over 160 works, including commentaries on the works of Aristotle, Plato, Euclid, and Ptolemy. He was praised as "Al-Mu'allim al-Sani" ("Second Teacher" — after Aristotle) and "Aristotle of the East." In the treatise "Maslag of the Inhabitants of the Virtuous City," he explained the origin of society by the need of people for one another and condemned a society based on seizure as an ignorant society. He was also known as a major musicologist for his "Great Book of Music."

## 4. Beruni and Ibn Sina

### Abu Rayhan Beruni (973-1048)

Beruni was born in the ancient capital of Khwarazm, Kat, and was educated by Abu Nasr ibn Iraq; he knew Arabic, Sogdian, Persian, Syriac, Greek, ancient Hebrew, and later Sanskrit. He was an active member of the Mamun Academy and an advisor to Khwarazmshah Mamun II; After the conquest of Khwarazm by Mahmud of Ghazni in 1017, he was taken to Ghazni.

| Production | Content |
|---|---|
| "Osar al-Baqiya" (1000) | "Monuments Left from Ancient Peoples" — Calendar and History |
| "India" (1030) | Indian Philosophy, Religion, Science, and Traditions |
| "The Law of Masud" | Encyclopedia of Astronomy and Mathematics |
| "Mineral Science" | A work of mineralogy that had no equal in its time |
| "Saidana" | Description of medicinal plants |
| "Geodesy," "Famous People of Khorezm" | Geodesy; History of Khorezm |

Beruni left behind more than 160 works and translations. American scientist J. Sarton called him "the first sage of his time in the world," and I.Yu. Krachkovsky believed that "it is easier to list the fields of science he was not interested in than those he was interested in."

### Abu Ali ibn Sina (980-1037)

Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, was born in the village of Afshona near Bukhara; At the age of 10, he knew the Quran by heart, and at the age of 16-17, he became a famous physician. From 1000, he worked at the Academy of Ma'mun in Khwarazm, then, fleeing from the persecution of Mahmud of Ghazni, lived in Jurjan, Hamadan (he was a minister), and Isfahan; He died in Isfahan on June 18, 1037. The number of his works exceeds 450, and about 160 have reached us. "Kitab al-kanun fit-tibb" ("The Canon of Medicine," 5 books) was the main medical manual in Eastern and Western universities for 800 years and was published more than 30 times in Latin. "Kitab ush-shifa" (22 volumes) covers logic, physics, mathematics, and metaphysics. Ibn Sina, author of the philosophical novels "Hayy ibn Yaqzan" and "Qissa Tayra," is considered one of the founders of the rubai genre in Persian poetry. He was honored with the titles "Sheikh ur-Rais" and "King of Doctors"; one of his students is Omar Khayyam. Carl Linnaeus named the evergreen plant "Avicennium" in honor of Ibn Sina.

### Abu Abdullah al-Khwarizmi (d. 997)

Abu Abdullah al-Khwarizmi, the secretary of the Samanid vizier al-Utbi, is the author of the encyclopedic work "Mafotih al-Ulum" ("Keys to the Sciences"), which covers almost all fields of science of his time.

## 5. Literature and Linguistics

### Abu Abdullah Rudaki (860–941)

Rudaki, born in the village of Panjrudak near Samarkand, lived in the palace of Nasr II (914-943). He was blind; in his old age, he was persecuted and died in poverty in his own village. A thousand verses have reached us ("Modari May," "Dar Vasfi Bukhoro," "Karilikdan Shikoyat"). He praised friendship: "If the joy of the world is gathered, it cannot be higher than the meeting of friends." Rudaki was also a skilled musician who played the rud and tanbur.

### Yusuf Khass Hajib (XI century)

Yusuf Hos Hajib, who was born in 1016-1018 in the capital of the Karakhanids, Balasagun, completed the poem "Kutadgu Bilig" ("Knowledge That Brings Happiness") in 1069/70 and dedicated it to the Karakhanid ruler. This work was a book on the ethics of state administration for rulers and beks and was known by the names "Shahnameh al-Turki" and "Adab ul-Muluk."

### Mahmud al-Kashgari (XI century)

Mahmud al-Kashgari completed the "Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk" in 1074/75. He traveled to territories inhabited by Turkic tribes and studied dialects. The work explains more than 7,500 Turkic words and expressions, provides approximately 300 proverbs and over 700 poetic excerpts, and includes a world map in the shape of a circle with Central Asia in the center. This work is an invaluable source in studying the history of Turkic languages.

## 6. Khorezm Mamun Academy

In 995, the Emir of Gurganj, Ma'mun ibn Muhammad, liquidated the Afrigiid dynasty and moved the capital from Kat to Gurganj. Under his successors Ali ibn Ma'mun (997-1009) and Ma'mun ibn Ma'mun, Gurganj became a major scientific center in the East. From 1004, the "Dor ul-Hikma va Maorif" ("Collection of Scholars") was fully formed here, which is conventionally referred to in science as the Khorezm Mamun Academy.

| Scholar | Region |
|---|---|
| Abu Rayhan Beruni | Astronomy, Geography, History, Mineralogy |
| Abu Ali ibn Sina | Medicine, Philosophy |
| Abu Nasr ibn Iraq | Mathematics, Astronomy |
| Abu Sahl Masihi | Medicine |
| Abulkhair Hammar | Medicine, Philosophy |
| Abu Mansur al-Saalibi | Literature |

Nizami Aruzi Samarkandi writes in "Chahor Makola": "Many philosophers and scholars gathered in the palace... All of them were fully provided with life's necessities, were friends with one another, and relaxed with conversations and correspondence. According to Saalibi, there were about 200 Arabist writers in Gurganj. The Academy worked in fields such as mathematics, astronomy, medicine, philosophy, history, linguistics, chemistry, music, and geodesy. After the conquest of Khwarazm by Mahmud of Ghazni in 1017, the scholars were taken to Ghazni. On November 11, 1997, by Presidential Decree, the Ma'mun Academy was reorganized in Khiva; On November 2, 2006, the 1000th anniversary of the academy was celebrated with the participation of UNESCO.

## 7. Islamic Sciences: Great Muhaddiths and Faqihs

In the 9th-12th centuries, Bukhara became famous under the name "Qubbat ul-Islam." Almost all authors of the six most authoritative collections of authentic hadiths in the Islamic world were originally from Central Asia.

### Imam al-Bukhari (810-870)

Imam al-Bukhari, who was born in Bukhara, went to Hijaz at the age of 16 and studied in Mecca, Medina, Damascus, Cairo, Basra, and Baghdad; He knew about 600,000 hadiths by heart. When the Emir of Bukhara demanded that he come to the palace to teach, he replied: "I do not neglect science and do not bring it to the doors of rulers," for which he was exiled from the city and died in the village of Hartang near Samarkand. "Al-Jome' as-Sahih" ("Sahih al-Bukhari") consists of 7275 (without repetition 4000) authentic hadiths and is the second source in Islam after the Quran. He was awarded the title of "Amir of the Faithful in Hadith Science." In 1998, the 1225th anniversary was celebrated, and the mausoleum was rebuilt.

### Imam at-Termizi (824-892)

Imam at-Termizi, who was born in Termez, was a student and associate of Imam al-Bukhari. Al-Bukhari said to him: "The profit I received from you is greater than the profit you received from me." His work "Al-Jami" ("Sunan at-Termizi") is one of the six authentic collections, and "Ash-Shama'il an-Nabawiya" consists of 408 hadiths about the Prophet's virtues.

### Other scientists

Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 945) from the village of Moturid in Samarkand was recognized as the founder of the Sunni sect of Moturidiyya, the "Imam al-Mutakallimin." Qaffal al-Shashi (903-976) was a Tashkent locksmith and faqih whose name is associated with the Tashkent complex "Hastimom" ("Hazrati Imam"). Mahmud al-Zamahshari (1075-1144), a native of the Khwarazmian village of Zamahshar, earned the nickname "Jorullah" for living in Mecca; his tafsir "Al-Kashshaf" and Arabic grammar "Al-Mufassal" are known, and he was glorified as "Honorable Khwarazm," "Teacher of Arabs and non-Arabs." The work "Hidoya" (1178, Samarkand) by Burhanuddin al-Marginani (1123-1197) was the primary fiqh manual in the Hanafi madhhab and was practiced in Central Asia until the 1930s.

## 8. Tariqats of Sufism

In Transoxiana, Sufism began to spread from the 10th century; its social core consisted of artisans and small traders. The main role was played by the teachings of Yusuf Hamadani, who built a khanqah in Bukhara and raised 213 famous sheikhs. Two schools originated from him; besides these, Najmiddin Kubro also founded a separate school.

| Tariqat | Founder | Peculiarity |
|---|---|---|
| Yassaviya | Ahmed Yasawi (born in Yassi in 1105) | "Devoni Hikmat" in the Turkic language; complete obedience to the sheikh; "Sultan ul-Orifin" |
| Khojagon (later Naqshbandiya) | Abdulhalik Gijduvani (1103-1179) | "Zikri hufiya" (heart recording) |
| Kubroviya | Najmuddin Kubro (1145-1221) | Rule 10; rejects asceticism and calls for the defense of the motherland |

According to Ahmad Yassawi, there is no tariqat without sharia, no enlightenment without tariqat, no truth without enlightenment—they complement each other. Najmuddin Kubro remained faithful to his idea and died in 1221 while defending Urgench from the Mongols.

## 9. Architecture and Art

### Architecture

Clay mortar, raw and fired bricks, ganch, and wood were used in construction; From the 10th century, cube-shaped buildings covered with a dome became widespread. The Mausoleum of Ismail Samani (Bukhara) is a unique structure built of fired brick, decorated equally on all four sides. The Minaret of Kalyan was built in 1127 by the Karakhanid Arslan Khan. The minarets of Vobkent and Jarkurgan (1108-1109), the Raboti Malik caravanserai, the Magoki Attoron mosque, the magnificent palace of the rulers of Termez with an area of about 10 hectares, and the mausoleum of Fakhriddin Razi in Gurganj are also architectural monuments of this period. Water facilities were also built: the Khanbandin reservoir held 1.5 million m3 of water; its construction required mathematical calculations of water pressure and earthquake forces.

### Art &amp; Music

In painting, the transition from depicting people and animals to complex geometric and floral patterns; calligraphy was widely used in the decoration of buildings. Musical instruments included the ud, tanbur, kobyz, rubob, nay, surnay, and karnay; Rudaki's contemporary Abu Hafz Sogdi created a stringed instrument called the shahrud.

## 10. The Role of the Renaissance Heritage in World Civilization

### The Significance of Scientific Heritage

The Eastern Renaissance of the 9th-12th centuries was an important stage in the development of humanity. Scientific works created during this period reached medieval Europe through translations into Latin and influenced the development of science and education in Western Europe, and subsequently the formation of the European Renaissance. Algebra and the counting system of al-Khwarizmi, astronomy of al-Farghani, research of Beruni on geodesy and natural sciences, "The Canon of Medicine" of Ibn Sina have been taught in the East and the West for centuries. Therefore, historically, the heritage of our ancestors is not limited to the history of a single region, but is valued as an integral part of universal human civilization.

### Ascent and its decline

The flourishing of Renaissance culture was the result of political stability, the rulers' patronage of science, and dialogue between different cultures. At the same time, the fate of scientific centers depended on the political situation: the conquest of Khwarazm by Mahmud Ghaznavi put an end to the activities of the Mamun Academy, and the 13th-century Mongol invasion led to the destruction of many cities, libraries, and scientific schools. However, the heritage of ancestors was preserved and revived at a new stage during the Timurid era (XIV-XV centuries) thanks to the activities of Amir Temur and Mirzo Ulugbek, and Alisher Navoi.

### Attitude towards inheritance

Today, the names of Renaissance scholars play an important role in realizing the historical identity and national pride of the Uzbek people. Studying, translating, and widely disseminating their works, celebrating their anniversaries at the international level, and constructing scientific and educational complexes are expressions of respect for the heritage of our ancestors. At the same time, this heritage should serve as an example of striving for science, hard work, and humanism for the younger generation.

## Conclusion

In the 9th-12th centuries, Central Asia became one of the leading centers of world culture. As a result of extensive ties within the caliphate, the stability of independent states, the rulers' patronage of science, and the call of Islam to science, scientific centers such as the "Bayt ul-Hikma" in Baghdad and the Khorezm Mamun Academy emerged. Al-Khwarizmi laid the foundations of algebra and algorithm, al-Farghani of astronomy, Farabi of philosophy, Beruni of encyclopedic research, and Ibn Sina of medicine; their works were taught in European universities for centuries. The great representatives of Islamic sciences were Imam al-Bukhari, at-Termezi, al-Maturidi, al-Marginani, and the great representatives of Sufism were Yassavi, Gijduvani, and Najmuddin Kubro. This heritage of our ancestors is an integral part of the universal human civilization.

## Key concepts

- **Eastern Renaissance (Renaissance)** — the period of flourishing science and culture in the 9th-12th centuries.
- **"Bayt ul-Hikma"** — "House of Sages" in Baghdad, Ma'mun Academy.
- **Ma'mun Academy** — a scientific center that operated in Gurganj from 1004 to 1017.
- **Zij** — a collection of astronomical tables.
- **Muhaddith** — a scholar in the science of hadith; faqih - Islamic jurist.
- **Sufism, tariqat** — the doctrine of Sufism and its branches.
- Algorithm is a scientific term derived from the name of al-Khwarizmi.
