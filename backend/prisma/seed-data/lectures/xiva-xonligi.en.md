## 1. Sources and Formation of the Khanate

### Sources

The works of Abulgazi Bahodir Khan's "Shajarai Turk," Bayani's "Shajarai Khwarazmshahi," Muhammad Vafo Karmanagi's "Tarihi Rahimkhani," Muhammad Rizo Agahi's "Jame al-Vokeoti Sultoni," and Munis and Agahi's "Firdaws ul-Iqbal" are of great importance in studying the history of the Khiva Khanate. This period was studied by S.P. Tolstov, Ya.G. Gulyamov, M. Yuldashev, P.P. Ivanov, and other scholars; the memoirs of Russian ambassadors and travelers who visited the khanate are also an important source. The peculiarity of Khiva historiography lies in the fact that its main works were written at the khan's court, often by the khans themselves or by their authorized secretaries.

### Formation of the khanate

At the end of the 15th century, Khwarazm belonged to the Timurid Sultan Husayn Bayqara, who was ruled by Chin Sufi. In 1505, Muhammad Shaybani Khan captured Urgench after a ten-month siege. After the death of Shaybani Khan (1510), Khwarazm passed into the hands of the Iranian Shah Ismail Safavi, and Iranian darughs were appointed in the cities. Public discontent with the tyranny of the "Kyzylbash" intensified. The movement was led by the city's qadi, Wazir Umar Sheikh; the rebels exterminated the Iranian governors in Wazir, Urgench, Khiva, and Hazarasp.

In 1511, the descendants of Sheikh Said Ata invited Elbars Khan from the Dasht-i Qipchaq Uzbeks, a descendant of Berke Sultan, to the throne. Elbars Khan expelled the Iranians, established an independent khanate, and expanded the territory through Southern Turkmenistan, Saraxs, Mangyshlak, Abulkhan, and Durun. This dynasty is also known in history as the Arabshahs. Thus, the Khiva Khanate entered history as a state formed as a result of the local population's uprising against foreign rule.

## 2. Political processes in the 16th-17th centuries. Abulgazi Khan

### The struggle for the throne

After the death of Elbars Khan (1518), separatism intensified. The ruler of Bukhara, Ubaydulla Khan, made a campaign against Khwarazm in 1537; Avanesh Khan was killed in the battle of Biyatkir. However, in 1538, the Bukhara army was defeated at Kardaronkhos. After this, the rulers of Urgench, Kat, Khiva, and Hazarasp acted independently; some cities were governed by two rulers simultaneously. According to Abulgazi, dozens of khans changed in the 16th century within a short period.

### Hajim Khan and the transfer of the capital

Hajimkhan (Haji Muhammadkhan; 1558-1593, 1598-1602) put an end to internecine wars. In sources, he is described as an "experienced, just, pious ruler"; attention to irrigation, agriculture, crafts, and trade intensified. Under him, the capital was moved from Urgench to Khiva. This was due to two main reasons: first, the Amu Darya changed its course and ceased to flow into the Caspian, causing water shortages in the vicinity of Urgench; second, the political and economic status of Khiva increased, and it became a major trading center. After this, the state became known as the Khiva Khanate (the resettlement took place in stages and was completed under Abulgazi Khan). In 1593, Abdullah Khan II captured Khwarazm, and Hajim Khan fled to Iran to the court of Shah Abbas. After Abdullah Khan's death in 1598, he returned and restored power.

### Arab Muhammad Khan and Isfandiyar Khan

During the reign of Arab Muhammad Khan (1602-1621), conspiracies for the throne continued. His seven sons also claimed the throne; In 1621, his sons Habash and Elbars Sultan defeated their father, blinded him, and in 1623, killed him and his family. Isfandiyar fled to Iran, and Abulgazi to Bukhara. Isfandiyar Khan gathered an army from Turkmen clans, defeated his brothers in the 23-day battle for Urgench, and became khan (1623-1643). He relied on the Turkmen leaders, giving them high positions and privileges; he exterminated the Nayman clan. As a result, the dissatisfaction of Uzbek clans intensified, especially the influence of the Uzbeks of the Aral Sea region (where the Kungrats held a high position).

### Abulgazi Khan (1643-1663)

Abulgazi Bahadur Khan was a prominent statesman and historian. His life was difficult: for more than twenty years, he lived in exile in Bukhara under the patronage of Imamquli Khan, at the court of the Kazakh Sultan Eshim Khan, and in Tashkent. Isfandiyar Khan arrested him and exiled him to Iran; he lived in captivity for ten years in the Tabarak fortress near Isfahan and fled in 1639. Having visited the Mangyshlak Turkmens and the Kalmyk Khan, the Uzbeks of the Aral Sea region elevated him to the rank of khan in 1643. As Academician B. Akhmedov wrote: "Abulgazi sat in the khan's post for about twenty years. But he did not enjoy life like other rulers.

Abulgazi Khan carried out an administrative reform: he removed the Turkmen leaders from governance and appointed the heads of Uzbek clans to their positions; 360 representatives received positions at the court, 32 of whom were the most influential and constituted the Khan's closest council. He mounted the Uzbek tribes, divided them into four groups, and settled them from Doyahotun to the Aral Sea. In 1645-1650, he conducted campaigns against the Turkmens. In 1646, the Urgench canal was dug and the New Urgench fortress was built; In 1648, the Gaziabad Canal was dug. Khan wrote the medical works "Manofi' ul-inson" (1657), the historical works "Shajarai Turk," and "Shajarai Tarokima."

During his lifetime, Abulgazi Khan transferred the throne to his son Anusha Khan (1663-1686). Anusha Khan made campaigns against Bukhara, Samarkand, and Khurasan, briefly captured Samarkand in 1685, recited a khutba in his name, and twice captured Mashhad. The Shahabad, Yarmish, and Gaziabad canals were constructed. During the reigns of Abulgazi Khan and Anusha Khan, the authority of the central government strengthened. In 1686, Anusha Khan was killed as a result of a conspiracy led by the Mangyt Odinakul Atalyk.

## 3. Crisis in the 18th century

In 1690, the Khan of Bukhara, Subhankuli Khan, placed his man Shahniyaz on the Khiva throne and effectively subordinated the khanate to Bukhara. The rulers of the regions ceased to obey the khan, and the khan turned into a puppet. In 1700, Shahniyaz, to rid himself of the influence of Bukhara, sent a letter to Peter I requesting that Khiva be admitted to Russia; although the letter was received, it held no significance in the context of political struggle.

Under Shergazi Khan (1715-1728), the Kungrat, Mangit, Khojeyli, and Qipchaq clans of the Aral Sea region fought for independence; the Mangit biy Sheralibiy declared the Aral Sea region independent. In 1717, a 6,000-strong expedition led by Bekovich-Cherkassky, sent by Peter I, was destroyed by the cunning of Shergazi Khan in a single night. In 1728, the khan was killed as a result of a conspiracy by a guard consisting of Persian slaves.

### Nadir Shah's campaign

During the reign of the Kazakh Sultan Elbars Khan (1728-1740), military power weakened. In 1740, Nadir Shah attacked Khiva: in the battle of Hazarasp, the Khivans were defeated, the Mangyt beks sided with Nadir Shah, and Elbars Khan was executed. Karmanagi notes that the Khwarazmians defended their homeland desperately. In 1741, an uprising against the Iranians broke out in Khiva, and Nadir Shah's governors were killed, but Nadir Shah's son Nasrullah launched a new campaign.

In subsequent years, the Kazakh sultans and khans appointed to Bukhara changed frequently. Gaibkhan (1747-1757) collected an additional 40,000 tenge in taxes from the population in 1756 and became the cause of the uprising. In the 1860s, the influence of the Yavmut Turkmens intensified, and between 1767 and 1770, power was effectively in their hands; In 1770, Khiva, Hazarasp, and Khanka were plundered. According to Munis, a terrible famine began during this period, and people migrated to the Aral Sea region and Bukhara; Only 40 families remained in Khiva. This picture shows the depth of the state crisis.

## 4. The Qongrat dynasty

### Era of the Inaks

The status of the Kungrats began to rise during the reign of Anusha Khan. Eshmuhammadbiy was one of the most influential politicians under Shergazi Khan and Elbars Khan and was executed in the campaign of Nadir Shah. His son, Muhammad Amin, became a perceptive politician and was appointed to the position of inoq in 1763. Muhammad Amin Inaq (1763-1790) appointed loyal people to palace positions, took political, financial, and military power into his own hands, and relied on city merchants, artisans, and religious figures. In 1770, he repelled the Turkmens, and in 1782, the Bukharans, and took measures to develop agriculture, trade, and craftsmanship.

His son, Avaz-inak (1790-1804), continued the policy. In 1804, Avaz's son Eltuzar, having expelled the fictitious khan Abulgazi V, officially proclaimed himself khan, thereby formally establishing the rule of the Qongrat dynasty. Eltuzar Khan died in 1806 in a battle against Bukhara.

### Muhammad Rahim Khan I (1806-1825)

Muhammad Rahim Khan I completed the unification of the khanate and carried out administrative-political, economic, and military reforms. He granted positions and titles to influential representatives of Uzbeks, Turkmens, Kazakhs, and Karakalpaks. A permanent Council (Divan) was established under the palace, and the Sheikh-ul-Islam became a member of it. Taxes were streamlined, and a customs system was established. In 1811, Aral-Kungrad was conquered, and in 1812-1813, the Kazakh and Karakalpak auls on the banks of the Syr Darya were conquered. A struggle for Bukhara and Southern Turkmenistan began (1817 defeat at Chorjou), and the Marv oasis was captured.

### Allaquli Khan and his followers

Allakulykhan (1825-1842) made 7 campaigns against Bukhara and 5 against Khurasan. He rejected Russia's demands: the 5,000-strong detachment of Orenburg Governor-General V. Perovsky suffered a setback in the winter of 1839 and returned; The embassies of Nikiforov (1841) and Danilevsky (1842) also yielded no results. During his reign, canals and water structures were built. Rahimquli Khan (1842-1845) was killed in one of the battles in Khorasan. Muhammad Amin Khan (1845-1855) made ten campaigns against the uprisings in Marw and Saraxsa, and was killed along with fourteen princes and officials in the Battle of Saraxsa in 1855. Shortly thereafter, Sayyid Abdullah Khan and Kutlug Murad Khan were killed.

In conditions of famine and disease, Said Muhammad Khan (1856-1864) sought to resolve the uprisings peacefully and improve trade with Bukhara and Russia. After him, Muhammad Rahim Khan II (1864-1910), known by the pseudonym "Feruz," ascended the throne as the most enlightened Khan of Khiva. During his reign in 1873, the Khiva Khanate became a dependent state of the Russian Empire.

## 5. Territory, administrative structure, and population

### Territory

The borders of the khanate constantly changed depending on the political situation. Under Eltuzar Khan, the northern border was Aral-Kungrad, and the southern was Darganata; In the mid-19th century, according to Russian sources, the khanate stretched west to the Caspian Sea, and south through Marv to Iran. Socially and economically, the khanate was divided into two parts. The northern part stretched from Kunya Urgench to Khojeyli to the Aral Sea (Shumanay, Khojeyli, Kungrad) and was a sparsely populated, steppe zone. The southern part-Khiva, Khanka, Khazarasp, Urgench, Kat, and Koshkupir-was densely populated, with many canals and fertile lands.

### Administrative division

In the 16th-18th centuries, it was divided into provinces, and from the end of the 18th century, into 16 bekdoms and 2 naibdoms (Hazorasp, Gurlen, Khanka, Kukhna Urgench, Shovot, Tashauz, Urgench, Khojeyli, Kungrad, etc.). The city of Khiva was under the jurisdiction of the khan and the chief minister. Muhammad Rahim Khan I established first 15, and then 11 governorates; the governorates were divided into mosque-communities (totaling 1537). The capital at various times was Vazir, Kat, Kunya Urgench, and Khiva.

### Population

The population was ethnically diverse: descendants of the ancient Khwarazmians adopted the Turkic language under the influence of Turkic tribes; In the 16th-17th centuries, many tribes arrived from Dasht-i Qipchaq. In the sedentary oases, mostly Uzbeks lived; in the northeast, the Karakalpaks who migrated from the banks of the Syr Darya in the 18th century (for livestock breeding, fishing, and farming); in the steppe, the Kazakhs; and in the west and south, the semi-nomadic Turkmens. In the second half of the 19th century, Turkmens occupied the second place in terms of population, accounting for 25% of the population.

Various data exist regarding the population size: in 1819, Muraviev wrote 300,000; the scholar M. Yuldashev, based on archival sources, established that about 800,000 people lived there in the middle of the 19th century. More than 21,000 people lived in Khiva. Such differences indicate the need for a critical assessment of the sources.

## 6. State system, ranks, and titles

In the 18th century, there was no stable dynasty in Khiva: although the Chingizids ascended the throne, power remained in the hands of the Kungrat inaks. From 1804, only the Kungrats became khans. The khan held supreme administrative, political, and military powers.

### Supreme Council

Unlike Bukhara and Kokand, Khiva had a Supreme Council under the khan. It was established by Muhammad Rahim Khan I instead of the council of clan elders. The council consisted of an inaq, a shaykh-ul-islam, a naqib, a mutawalli, a mirab, a qazi, a devonbegi, a yasaulbashi, and others. Although it was an official advisory body, in practice it performed the functions of the supreme legislative, administrative, and judicial authority on internal and external issues; the final decision depended on the khan's will.

### Court titles and positions

Palace titles and positions included:

- **Inak** — the highest title, the khan's closest advisor appointed from the most powerful Uzbek clans;
- **Atalik** — clan elder, khan's advisor;
- **Biy** — head of a tribe or clan, a hereditary title;
- **Amir ul-Umara** — "emir of emirs," the highest military-administrative position;
- **Kushbegi** — finance and tax collection;
- **Mekhtar** — the head of the palace servants responsible for collecting land tax (solgut); effectively the vizier;
- **Parvanachi** — the bearer of complaints to the khan, the disseminator of decrees;
- **Eshikohashi** — Head of the Palace Gate Guard (sign — crescent moon);
- **Mahram** — the khan's trusted advisor.

The palace had a watchman, a bookkeeper, a sherbet maker, a karnay player, an ashmekhtar, and many other servants, all of whom received their salaries from the treasury.

### Military and Religious Positions

The highest military official was the Khan himself. There were the positions of yasaulbashi (two people — one yavmut, the other chaudur, the commander of the Turkmens), mingbashi, yuzbashi, ellikbashi, unbashi, and kutvol (fortress chief). The nukers were exempt from taxes; the Turkmens, Kazakhs, and Karakalpaks provided the khan with mandatory troops.

Among religious positions, the sheikh-ul-islam sat on the khan's right hand during ceremonies; the chancellery of the qazi-kalon was called Dar ul-qazo; there were also qazi-askar, mufti (There were 7 chief muftis in Khiva), a'lam (head of the muftis), rais (control over the rules of sharia, scales in the markets, attendance of children in schools), mutawalli (vaquf nazir), mudarris, and akhun. The textbook concludes that although Khiva's state structure was not at the level of Western Europe, it possessed a sufficiently orderly management system.

## 7. Land ownership, economy, and taxes

### Land ownership

There were three main forms of land ownership in Khiva. Tsarist (state) lands included irrigated lands, hills, pastures, and lakes; they were expanded through seized and confiscated lands, and 40-50% of the harvest was collected as a tax. Private (mulk) lands belonged to the khan, his relatives, officials, and wealthy individuals, accounting for more than half of the irrigated land in the first half of the 19th century (some owners had tens of thousands of tanobs). In the mid-19th century, waqf lands amounted to 170-175 thousand tanobs.

Landless peasants were tenants and, depending on whose land they worked, were called bevatan (state land), koranda (owned land), and waqfkor (waqf land).

### Agriculture and animal husbandry

Agriculture relied on canals dug from the lower reaches of the Amu Darya, but it was not developed at the level of Bukhara. Wheat (especially from Pitnak to Shahabbaz), rice, cotton (Gurlan, Urgench, Khiva, Khanka, Shahabbaz), sesame, peas, and mung beans; Khorezm melons were known. Sericulture is poorly developed. In sedentary areas, livestock farming was weak, and the need for meat was met by nomadic Turkmens and Kazakhs.

### Taxes

With the reform of Muhammad Rahim Khan I, a cash tax-salgut-was introduced instead of kharaj as a product:

| Volume of land | Annual tax |
|---|---|
| more than 10 tanobs | 3 gold (54 coins) |
| 5-10 tanobs | 2 Gold (36 Coins) |
| up to 5 tanobs | 1 Gold (18 Coins) |

The Karakalpaks and Kungrats paid an annual "tax cut" of 24,500 tillas. Livestock breeders first paid zakat in kind (one head per 40 heads), and then in cash. The most difficult of the additional obligations were excavations, flight, and begor, which meant annual free labor for 12 days on canals, dams, fortresses, roads, and bridges.

## 8. Crafts, trade, and cities

Domestic craftsmanship prevailed in semi-nomadic households. Textile (silk, semi-silk, cotton fabrics), pottery, copper, foundry, blacksmithing, and carpentry production are developed; Khiva carpets were popular in the domestic and foreign markets. The change in the course of the Amu Darya at the end of the 16th century also changed the external trade routes: in trade with Russia, the routes through the lower reaches of the Volga, and from the middle of the 18th century through Orenburg, acquired significant importance. In Astrakhan, Khivan merchants were granted privileges. Cotton and silk fabrics, carpets, fruits and karakul, gold and silver coins, metals, sugar, tea, and crystal vessels were exported to Russia.

Of the main trade centers, New Urgench was surrounded by a wall, had 1,500 apartments, and about 300 shops; Urgench merchants traded in the markets of Astrakhan, Orenburg, Nizhny Novgorod, Moscow, Kashgar, Mashhad, and Kabul. There were 8 mosques, 8 madrasas, and about 400 shops in the city of Hazarasp, and up to 3,000 houses in the vicinity of Gurlan. Khiva has been the capital since the beginning of the 17th century, and Russian travelers wrote that its markets are no worse than those of Bukhara and Kokand.

## 9. Cultural life and architecture

### Formation

In the 19th century, there were about 1,500 schools and 130 madrasas in the khanate; 22 madrasas operated in Khiva alone (Muhammad Rahim Khan, Shergazi Khan, Allaquli Khan, Khoja Mahram, and others).

### Literature and historiography

In the 18th century, Nurmuhammad Garib Andalib (1710-1770) created the poems "Yusuf and Zulaikha" and "Layli and Majnun." In the 19th century, Muhammad Reza Ogahi (1809-1874) became known as a poet, historian, and translator; he has a divan titled "Tavizul Oshiqon." In oral folk art, the "Gorogly" cycle, the dastans "Tahir and Zuhra," and "Oshiq Garib and Shahsanam" were developed

Abulgazi Khan's works "Shajarai Turk" and "Shajarai Tarokima" are important historical works written in the Uzbek language. Shermuhammad Munis (1778-1829) wrote "Firdaws ul-Iqbal" at the behest of Muhammad Rahim Khan; it was continued by Agahi's nephew with events up to 1872. Bayani's work "Shajarai Khwarazmshahi" is also a valuable source.

### Architecture

Khorezm possesses a unique architectural school: the structures are distinguished by their exquisite decor and elegant patterns. Monuments in Khiva are mainly located in Ichan-Kala; Dishan-kala, the khan's summer residence, is also a rare example. Cities such as New Urgench, Khazarasp, Tashauz, Gurlen, and Kungrad were surrounded by defensive walls.

## Conclusion

The Khiva Khanate emerged in 1511 as a result of the struggle against Iranian rule. The change in the course of the Amu Darya led to the relocation of the capital to Khiva. In the 16th-18th centuries, the struggle for the throne and the attacks of neighboring states and nomadic tribes led the khanate to crisis several times; Under Abulgazi Khan and Anusha Khan, central power strengthened. From the end of the 18th century, power was stabilized by the Kongrat monks, and from 1804, by the Kongrat dynasty; The reforms of Muhammad Rahim Khan I united the state. The khanate possessed a unique Supreme Council, a developed system of ranks, the Khorezm architectural school, and rich historiographical traditions, and its history is an integral part of the history of Uzbekistan's statehood.

## Key concepts

- The Arabshahs are the descendants of Elbars Khan, who ruled from 1511.
- The Qongrat dynasty-officially ruling since 1804.
- **Inak** — the highest title in Khiva; the Kungrat Inaks - the current rulers from 1763 to 1804.
- The Supreme Council was a consultative and, in fact, the highest governing body under the khan.
- The mosque is the lowest administrative unit.
- Bevatan, koranda, and waqfkor are categories of tenant farmers.
- Salgut is a land tax in monetary form introduced by Muhammad Rahim Khan I.
- **Excavations, begor** — compulsory free public works.
- Ichan-Kala and Dishan-Kala are the inner and outer fortresses of Khiva.
