## 1. Sources and the rise of the Manghits to power

### Sources

The works of Abdurahman Tale's "Tarikh-i Abulfayzkhani," Muhammad Wafa Karminagi's "Tuhfai Khani," Mirzo Abdulazim Sami's "Tarikh-i Salotini Mangitiya," Mirzo Salimbek's "Tarikh-i Salimi," and Ahmad Donish's "Risola-i Tarikh-i Amironi Mangit" are of great importance for the history of the period. Issues of state administration are particularly highlighted in the work "Majma al-Arqam" by Mirza Badi Diwan. The topic has been studied by A.A. Semenov, O. Sukhareva, R.G. Mukminova, G.A. Agzamova, and other scientists. Since most of these works were written by order or under the patronage of the Manghit rulers, a critical analysis of them is required.

### The rise of the Manghits

During the reign of the last Ashtarkhan, Abulfayzkhan, central power weakened, and Uzbek clans began to declare themselves independent in various regions. Among them, the leaders of the Mangit clan, Muhammad Hakimbiy Atalik (died in 1743), and his son Muhammad Rahimbiy, held a special place. In 1745, Nadir Shah sent a large army to Bukhara led by Muhammad Rahimbi. Rahimbiy suppressed the uprisings in Miyankal and Shahrisabz, appointed his relatives and associates to high positions, and strengthened his position in the capital.

Shortly after the assassination of Nadir Shah in 1747, Abulfayz Khan was executed at the behest of Rahimbiy; his son Abdulmumin was also killed in 1748, and the young Ubaydullah Sultan was only nominally installed on the throne. In 1756, four Uzbek clans-the Mangyt, Chinese-Kipchak, Bahrin, and Saray-with the consent of major officials, clergy, and clan elders, seated Muhammad Rahim Khan on the throne. Thus, the reign of the Manghit dynasty began.

## 2. Muhammad Rahimkhan and Daniyalbiy atalyk

### Muhammad Rahim Khan (1756-1758)

It was beneficial for Rahimbiy, who was not a Chingizid, to marry Abulfayz Khan's daughter. First and foremost, it relied on the Mangyt clan: after each Ramadan, Mangyt officials received remuneration ranging from 30 to 100 coins. Disobedient tribes were punished and relocated to other places (seven clans from Miyankala, barkutes from Nur, durmans from Kobadiyan, kenegases from Shahrisabz, and others).

Muhammad Rahim Khan pursued the policy of a centralized state. He subjugated such rebellious regions as Miyankal, Nurata, Urgut, Kabadiyan, and Boysun, as well as the upper reaches of Zarafshon, Jizzakh, and Zomin. By 1758, ambassadors arrived from Tashkent, Kokand, Marw, Balkh, and Kunduz, who recognized his authority. The Dargom canal, destroyed by the war, was dug again, the defensive wall of Bukhara was reinforced, and the roads were repaired.

### Daniyalbiy atalyk (1758-1785)

After Rahim Khan's death, his young grandson Faziltura was installed on the throne, but power passed into the hands of his uncle, Daniyalbi Atalik. He did not lay claim to the khanate, but first proclaimed Faziltura and then Abulgazi as khans, considering himself their governor. Its period is explained by the weakening of central power. Great unrest began in Miyankal, Shahrisabz, Kitab, Sherabad, Boysun, and Hisar; The emirs of the Yuz, Kenegas, Barkut, Bahrin, and Saray clans exiled to Hisar planned to overthrow the Manghits. The 1771 uprising in Shahrisabz and Huzor shook the foundations of the state. Taxes rose, the people's lives deteriorated; Daniyalbi handed over the power to Nizamiddin the Qazi-Kalan and Davlat Qushbegi, while he himself indulged in debauchery.

In 1785, an uprising against Daniyalbi broke out in Bukhara. Although he was crushed, Daniyalbiy was forced to hand over the throne to his son Shahmurad.

## 3. Amir Shahmurad's reforms

Amir Shahmurod (1785-1800) grew up pious from a young age, graduated from a madrasah, and became a dervish preacher. He removed Khan Abulgazi and declared himself absolute ruler with the title of "amir." Expressing sympathy for the demands of the rebels, he abolished heavy taxes; he executed Davlat Kushbegi and Nizomiddin Kazikalan, who were known for their bribery. He remained in history under the name "Amiri Ma'sum" ("The Sinless Emir").

### Four reforms

Under Shahmurad, four important reforms were carried out in the financial, judicial, administrative, and military spheres. According to the financial (money) reform, coins were minted from pure silver and pure gold without any impurities, differing from the previous ones in value, quality, and type; the population was allowed to mint coins of the same size from their own silver and gold at the state mint. Within the framework of the judicial reform, the affairs of the judicial collegium were managed by the Emir himself, and a special code of judicial laws was developed, based on which all regional, district, and bek's judges operated.

In the tax system, tax types such as yorgu, boj, tarkh, tushman, yasak, oliq, and the craftsmen's tax were abolished, the kharaj was reduced, and only taxes corresponding to Sharia were left. But the newly introduced "road" tax became a heavy burden on the people. According to the military reform, the lands belonging to major commanders were seized, and a monthly salary for military personnel was introduced from the income derived from them. These measures served to limit the independence of the military aristocracy.

### Lifestyle and politics

Shahmurad led an ascetic lifestyle, wearing one colorful robe, a shirt made of calico, and a simple calico turban every year. He prohibited alcoholic beverages, smoking, singing, tightrope walking, buffoonery, and puppetry, closed entertainment venues, and, conversely, restored waqfs, improved mosques and madrasas, and resolutely fought against theft and robbery. Water facilities have been repaired, and bridges have been built.

In 1786, the campaigns against the defiant Karmana, and subsequently against Shahrisabz and Khujand, were successfully completed. Abroad, he fought with the Afghan Emir Timur Shah for lands on the right bank of the Amu Darya; according to the treaty, the Amu Darya was defined as the border of the two states, and Maymana and Andhud were recognized as belonging to Bukhara. As a result, in a country that had been in chaos for decades, relative tranquility was established, internal and external trade developed, the population's lives improved, and the state's prestige increased.

## 4. Period of Amir Haidar and Amir Nasrullah

### Amir Haidar (1800-1826)

Haydar, who was the ruler of Qarshi in his father's time, was raised on white felt, but he received the title of "Amir al-Mu'minin" rather than a khan. He studied for 10 years in a madrasah in Bukhara and was well-versed in religious and secular sciences. He brought many books from Istanbul, Kabul, and other cities, and sometimes taught in madrasas himself. In Bukhara, he built the madrasas of his mother, Chor Minor, and Khalifa Niyazkul, and in Qarshi, the madrasas of Ali and Mir Muhammad.

Under Haydar, the entire population of Bukhara was mobilized in 1806, and the army of Khiva Khan Eltuzar was defeated. In 1807 and 1810, the Khan of Kokand, Alim Khan, made raids on Ura-Tyube, and there were campaigns against Jizzakh and Zomin. Additional taxes were introduced for military expenses, and uprisings occurred in Miyankal, Samarkand, and Urgut between 1821 and 1825. As a result of the 1817 campaign, Kunduz, Badakhshan, and Maymana passed into the possession of Bukhara. Haydar even turned to the Ottoman Sultan Mahmud II for help. Under him, 50 Sarbaz detachments of 800 men each and an artillery group of 250 men were formed; the regular infantry numbered more than 40,000 people.

### Amir Nasrullah (1826-1860)

After Haydar's death, his eldest sons, Husayn (two and a half months) and Umar (four months), ruled for a short time and were killed by his brother, the ruler of Qarshi, Nasrullah. Amir Nasrullah pursued an extremely harsh policy to end political fragmentation and aristocratic tyranny, earning the nickname "butcher emir"; according to sources, between 50 and 100 people were executed daily at the beginning of his reign. At the same time, for his bravery, he was addressed as a "bahadur."

Shahrisabz was the largest rebellious region to which dissatisfied officials fled. In the war that began in 1832, Nasrullah made 32 campaigns and only in 1856 subjugated Shahrisabz and Kitab. In 1842, he occupied Kokand; The Khan of Kokand, Muhammad Ali Khan, his mother, the poetess Nadirabegim, and others were killed. The Khan of Khiva retreated due to the attack of Allakulykhan. He made a campaign against Khiva, besieged Hazarasp, but was defeated; In 1843, he captured Marw. Continuous wars with Kokand over Ura-Tyube and Khujand led to the destruction of the cities.

In 1837, a permanent army consisting of sarbazes was established in the emirate. In Bukhara, 9 madrasas were built, the Khalifa Khudoydod complex was completed (1855), and irrigation ditches, tims, caravanserais, baths, and bridges were built. According to sources, Amir Nasrullah was the last active independent ruler of Bukhara. During the reign of Amir Muzaffar (1860-1885), who succeeded him to the throne, the emirate became a dependent state of the Russian Empire.

## 5. Anglo-Russian Competition and Bukhara

In the first half of the 19th century, English-Russian competition for Central Asia intensified. Both countries sought to expand their colonies, obtain raw materials and markets, and strengthen their influence in the East. In 1825, the Englishman W. Murcroft arrived in Bukhara as a "merchant" with 70 people and 80 trunks of goods, met with the Kushbegi, and attempted to create a group sympathetic to England, but Russia expressed its dissatisfaction. In 1826, Russia sent an expedition led by Colonel F. Berg to Ustyurt, which studied the routes leading to the khanates.

In 1832, Lieutenant A. Burns, who introduced himself as an Armenian merchant, arrived in Bukhara and gathered much information. Cheap English goods began to enter the emirate through Indian merchants. In 1839, the Anglo-Afghan War began; English intelligence sent spies from Herat to Bukhara. Stoddart and Conolly were later executed by Emir Nasrullah. In 1839, Russia sent an expedition led by V.A. Perovsky to Khiva; in 1841, the embassy of mining engineer K. Butenev spent 8 months in Bukhara gathering information, and the Aral and Kopal fortifications were built. The textbook concludes that the backwardness of the khanates, their isolation from world development, and internal wars were equally beneficial for England and Russia.

## 6. Territory, administrative structure, and population

### Territory

In the middle of the 18th century, the rulers of Bukhara retained only Bukhara and its surroundings, as well as Kashkadarya and the Miyankal oasis. Under the Manghits, the territory expanded again: at the beginning of the 19th century, the emirate included the Zarafshan, Kashkadarya, and Surkhan oases, as well as Hisar, Khujand, Ura-Tyube, Panjakent, and part of Southern Turkmenistan from Charzhou to Murghab. The emirate bordered Iran, Afghanistan, Khiva, the Kazakh zhuzes, and the Khanate of Kokand.

### Administrative division

In the 50s of the 19th century, the emirate consisted of provinces ruled by rulers; later, instead of provinces, bekdoms emerged (there were 44 bekdoms during this period). According to "Majma al-Arqam," for the convenience of tax collection, there was a division by the area of irrigated land:

| Unit | Irrigated land |
|---|---|
| Region | 100 thousand tanobs |
| Hazara | 50 thousand tanobs |
| Half-desert | 25 thousand tanobs |
| Drunkard | 10-15 thousand tanobs |
| Old man | 400 tanobs |
| Sown area | 300 tanobs |

### Population

The population increased rapidly from the second half of the 18th century: more than 2 million people lived there at the beginning of the 19th century, and more than 2.5 million people in the 1950s. There were 60,000 people in Bukhara and more than 50,000 in Samarkand.

| Indicator | Distribution |
|---|---|
| Lifestyle | sedentary — 65%, semi-nomadic — 15%, nomadic — 20% |
| Classes | agriculture and animal husbandry — 85%, handicrafts and trade — 10%, non-permanent employment — 5% |
| Ethnic composition | Turkic peoples — 85%, Iranian peoples — 12%, others — 3% |

The majority of the population were Uzbeks. Tajiks lived in the cities of Samarkand, Bukhara, Nurata, Urgut, and Kitab, as well as in the mountainous regions; Turkmens on both banks of the Amu Darya; and Arabs in Qarshi and Sherabad. There were Jewish, Tatar, and Afshar mahallas in Bukhara; Jewish schools and synagogues operated in Bukhara and Samarkand. There were also Indians, Afghans, Russians, Armenians, and Persians; the local population treated them well. This testifies to the fact that the society of the emirate was multinational and multi-confessional.

## 7. State Administration, Positions, and Ranks

During the reign of the Manghits, no significant changes occurred in the state system; it differed little from the Shaybanid and Ashtarkhanid systems. Rahimbiy did not accept the title of khan and called himself "naib ul-hukumat" (deputy of the ruler); Shahmurad ascended the throne with the title of "amir." The last Emir, Sayyid Alim Khan, wrote that the emirs of Bukhara followed Sharia law in their governance; the ulema considered the Emir "the Caliph of the Prophet, the protector of Sharia law." According to Uzbek custom, the new ruler was seated on a white felt mat, and he was raised by sayids, khojas, and mullahs.

### Central Administration

The Emir possessed absolute legislative and executive power. State matters were decided in the Ark; a state council consisting of 5 to 20 officials was convened as needed. The administration was two-tiered: in the center were the emir and the court nobles, and locally were the governors of the regions (beks were appointed mainly from the Mangyt clan). The lowest level is the village communities, headed by elders.

### The main positions were:

- Kushbegi (kushbegiyi bolo) — the highest palace office, effectively the Prime Minister;
- Divanbegi — control over finances, income and expenses, as well as tax collection;
- Atalik — under Shahmurad, his powers were limited and focused on the distribution of Zarafshon waters and control over the Bukhara canal;
- Kushbegiyi Poyon (Miripoyon) — the Emir's second minister;
- Mirshabboshi — city order; mirab — water supply;
- The Chief Chairman (Rais-ush-shariat) is a semi-religious position responsible for monitoring Sharia and morality.

In Bukhara, the kushbegi, kazikalon, chief rais, and mirshabbashi were collectively called "chor hokim" ("four hokims"). In the palace, there were low-ranking positions such as parvanachi, shigaul, dastarkhonchi, and imomi jilov; for loyalty, titles such as dodokh, inoq, tuksabo, and eshikohashi were bestowed.

### Religious and Military Positions

The highest religious office was sheikh-ul-islam, and from the middle of the 19th century, its place was taken by the qazi-kalon. Civil, economic, and criminal cases were resolved by judges based on Sharia law. Officially, there were three religious titles: serap, sudur, and sadr. Military duties were performed by the qazi-askar and the mufti-askar. The highest military ranks were sarkarda, amiri lashkar, and dodkhoh, as well as kurganbegi, tokusbo, mingboshi (with the right to raise a banner), pansadboshi, yuzboshi, ellikboshi, and onboshi.

## 8. Land ownership, economy, trade, and taxes

### Land ownership

| Form | Content |
|---|---|
| Sultan's estate | State lands; emir — the largest landowner. It was increased through confiscated and inheritance-free lands; 40-50% of the harvest was obtained |
| Property | Private land, inherited without restrictions |
| Equitable property | Large property exempt from land tax by a ruler's decree |
| Kharaj property | Peasant land; can be sold or bequeathed; tax from 1/3 to 1/5 of the harvest |
| Waqf | The land and income of religious institutions were distributed to the mutavalli |
| Lonely | Common since the time of Nasrullah — confiscated lands were temporarily given to supporters |

The development of vacant lands (reeds, tugai, stony) was exempt from taxes for several years.

### Agriculture and animal husbandry

85% of the population was engaged in agriculture and animal husbandry. Wheat, barley, sorghum, melons, and vegetables have been planted; more than 10 varieties of grapes have been grown. High-quality rice was grown in Shahrisabz, Kattaqurgan, and Hisar. From the beginning of the 19th century, due to the sale of cotton to Russia, attention to cotton growing intensified: according to Russian sources, Bukhara cotton was the most expensive among the khanates. Tobacco cultivation is developed in Karshi and Miyankal, and silkworm and mulberry cultivation in the waters. The land was traditionally cultivated with a plow, a harrow, a ketmen, and a shovel. In livestock farming in the Bukhara and Qarshi steppes, karakul breeding (high demand in the Russian market), horse breeding (Karabair horses of Shahrisabz and Arabian horses of Qarshi), and camel breeding were developed.

### Craft

Craftsmen united into unions (union, workshop), which were headed by an elected chairman or elder. The textile industry produced calico, chintz, alacha, and silk fabrics; The gold-embroidered robes of Bukhara, the skullcaps of Shahrisabz, and the silk fabrics of Termez were famous. Pottery was developed in Bukhara, Samarkand, Qarshi, Shahrisabz, Urgut, and Gijduvan. In metalworking, blacksmithing, coppersmithing (copper was brought from Russia), cast iron, and bronzesmithing became widespread; jewelry, leatherworking, and woodcarving also developed.

### Trade

Bukhara, Samarkand, Termez, Shahrisabz, Karshi, and Kattaqurgan were the main trading centers; At the beginning of the 19th century, the significance of Karshi at the crossroads of caravan routes increased. There were three types of money in circulation: gold, silver, and copper. Khiva, Kokand, the Kazakh and Turkmen steppes, Afghanistan, Iran, India, Kashgar, and especially Russia (Orenburg, Astrakhan) played an important role in foreign trade. Leather, karakul, wool, cotton, silk, and fabrics were exported to Russia, while factory goods, cast iron, copper, and lead were imported. Precious stones, medicines, and cashmere fabrics were brought from India, and silk and porcelain from China (through Kokand).

### Taxes

Kharaj was the primary land tax, amounting to 1/3, 1/4, and sometimes 1/5 of the harvest. Tanobona (tanob puli, alaf puli) was harvested from garden and fodder lands; During Nasrullah's reign, a double payment for vegetable gardens was introduced. Zakat constituted one-fortieth of the value of property and was collected from livestock breeders in the form of products. The road was an emergency tax during wartime. In addition, there were payments: dallali, baj, su puli, takhtajoy, yaksara (one batman of grain for a pair of plough animals), and nimsara; those who did not participate in the hashar were fined bakipuli.

## 9. Cultural life

### Formation

Primary schools existed in all large villages and urban neighborhoods: they taught literacy using the Arabic alphabet, studying verses from the Quran and the basics of Sharia. Training in the madrasah lasted seven years in three stages: the lower step, the middle step, and the advanced one. Madrasas were maintained at the expense of waqf revenues and were usually two-storied; the first floor housed a mosque, a reading room, and a library, while the second floor housed student rooms. Alongside religious sciences, language, history, astronomy, logic, and geometry were taught, but secular education was lagging behind. According to N. Khanikov, there were 103 madrasas in Bukhara in the 1940s.

### Literature and historiography

Muhammadniyaz Nishati wrote the poem "Husnu Dil" (1778) and the work "Discussion of Birds." Mujrim Obid (1748-1818) condemned social injustice by writing 459 ghazals in Turkic and 206 in Persian. 3000 poems of the blind poet Volya (1770-1842) have reached us. Mirza Sodiq Munshi was a secretary in the palace of Amir Haydar and compiled the "Divan of Sodiq Munshi." The scholar, philosopher, and historian Ahmad Donish (1827-1897) worked as a calligrapher and artist in the palace library for 20 years. Historical works are mainly dedicated to the Manghit dynasty: "Tarikh-i Amir Haidar," "Tarikh-i Salotini Mangitiya," "Taj-i Tavorikh," and others.

### Music, Calligraphy, Architecture

At the end of the 18th century, "Shashmaqom" emerged in Bukhara: it consists of the Buzruk, Rost, Navo, Dugoh, Segoh, and Iroq maqoms and includes more than 250 melodies and songs. Calligraphy schools existed in Samarkand, Bukhara, and Shahrisabz (Mir Ma'sum Olamiyon, Mirzo Sodiq Jondori). Among the architectural monuments is the Khalifa Khudoydod complex (mosque, madrasa, mausoleum, sardoba; The Chor Minor Madrasah (1807), the Domullo Tursunjan Madrasah (1796-1797), and the Ali and Mir Muhammad Madrasas in Qarshi are of particular importance; covered markets, caravanserais, baths, and sardobas along roads were built in the cities.

## Conclusion

The Manghit dynasty, after the collapse of the Ashtarkhanids, attempted to restore central power. Muhammad Rahim Khan united the territory, fragmentation intensified during the reign of Daniyalbi, and Amir Shahmurad's financial, judicial, administrative, and military reforms ensured relative stability and economic growth. Amir Haydar and Amir Nasrullah fought to expand the territory and subdue the unruly provinces, but constant wars with neighboring khanates weakened the country. Economic crisis, political instability, and internal conflicts led to the conquest of large territories of the emirate by Russia in the second half of the 19th century; The emirs of Bukhara ruled as vassals of Russia until 1920.

## Key concepts

- **Manchu Dynasty** — a dynasty that ruled in Bukhara from 1756 to 1920.
- Amir Ma'sum is the nickname of Emir Shahmurad.
- **Chor Hokim** — Qushbegi, Kazikalon, Chief Chairman, and Mirshabbashi.
- Kushbegi, divanbegi, miripoyan, mirshab, mirab are the primary positions.
- **Bekstvo** — an administrative unit of the 19th century (44 units).
- **Mulki kharaj, mulki khurr, tanho, amlok, waqf** — forms of land ownership.
- **Kharaj, zakat, jol, tanobona, yaksara** — types of taxes.
- Shashmaqom is a classical musical cycle that emerged in Bukhara.
