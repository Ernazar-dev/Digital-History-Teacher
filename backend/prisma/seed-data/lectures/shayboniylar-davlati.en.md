# Uzbek statehood during the reign of the Shaybanid dynasty

> The lecture was prepared based on Topic 3 of the textbook "History of Uzbekistan" by B.J. Eshov and A.A. Odilov (Book II, 2020), as well as sources related to the Shaybanid period.

## 1. Sources and Emergence of the Shaybanid State

### Sources

The centuries-long reign of the Shaybanids has found its reflection in many historical works. Among them, Muhammad Salih's "Shaybani-name" and the work of the same name by Kamoliddin Binoi, Zahiriddin Muhammad Babur's "Boburname," Khondamir's "Habib al-Siyar," Muhammad Haydar Mirzo's "Tarihi Rashidi," Muhammad ibn Arab Qatağan's "Musahhir al-Bilad," Hafiz Tanish Bukhari's "Abdulla-name" ("Sharafname of the Shah"), and Fazlullah Ruzbehan's "Mehman-name of Bukhara" hold a special place. Since the authors of these sources hold different political views, it is necessary to study them in parallel. This era was studied by V. Abdullayev, B. Akhmedov, R. Mukminova, A. Muhammadjanov, G.A. Agzamova, and other scholars.

### Abulkhair Khan's State

At the beginning of the 14th century, the Golden Horde split into two parts, and the Ak-Orda was formed in the eastern part. In the mid-15th century, the fifth son of Jochi Khan, Abulkhair Khan (1428–1468), from the Shaybanid lineage, founded the state of the nomadic Uzbeks in Eastern Dasht-i Qipchaq. He captured the cities of the middle reaches of the Syr Darya and part of Khwarazm, threatened the borders of the Timurids, and helped Abu Sa'id Mirza come to power in 1451. The capital of the state was moved from Tur to Sygnak. The formation of Abulkhair Khan's state marked the beginning of the entry of nomadic Uzbeks into the political life of Maveraunnahr.

### Muhammad Shaybani Khan

Muhammad Shaybani Khan (Muhammad Shahbaht) was born in 1451. After the death of his father, Shahbudak, the eldest son of Abulkhair Khan (1459–1460), he and his brother Mahmud were placed in the care of the Uyghur Bayshaykh Kukeldash, and later under the patronage of Karachebek, the ruler of Turkestan and Otrar, Muhammad Mazid-tarkhan. The brothers lived in Bukhara, received education, and fell in love with poetry. Sources describe Shaybani Khan as a talented poet and commander, one of the most educated people of his time. For some time, he served the ruler of Bukhara, Darvish Muhammad Tarkhan, and in 1488, he seized power in Dasht-i Qipchaq.

### Situation in Maveraunnahr

In the last quarter of the 15th century, a struggle for the throne flared up between the Timurid princes. Fergana, Hisar, Samarkand, Bukhara, Tashkent, and Khwarazm considered themselves independent. The Timurid rulers only united temporarily when threatened by an enemy, each striving to preserve their territory. Constant wars, high taxes, and the excessive revelry of the nobility weakened the population's trust in the Timurids.

Local rulers utilized hired detachments of nomads to replenish the army. Shaybani Khan also led such groups at first, which allowed him to get to know the situation in Maveraunnahr more closely. As noted in the source, the "great and small Shaybanids" were nukers of the ruler of Bukhara, Abdul Ali Tarkhan. Thus, the success of the Shaybanids is explained not only by military power but also by the internal crisis of the Timurid state.

## 2. Muhammad Shaybani Khan's campaigns and his death

### The Conquest of Samarkand and Bukhara

The ruler of Tashkent, Mahmud Khan (1487–1503), attempted to make Shaybani Khan an ally in the struggle against the Timurids and handed over Otrar to him in 1495. In 1498, the attack on Samarkand ended in failure: two groups formed in the city: supporters of Babur and supporters of Shaybani Khan. Despite this, Shaybani Khan's subsequent campaigns were successful, and within a few years, he captured most of Mawarannahr.

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1500 (June) | After a three-day siege, the Bukharan nobility recognized Shaybani Khan's authority |
| 1500 | The Samarkand beks recognized Shaybani Khan; Muhammad Salih was given the title "Amir of Emirs and King of Poets." |
| 1500 (autumn) | Babur seized Samarkand |
| 1501 | Shaybani Khan seized Samarkand from Babur |
| 1503 | In the battle at the Arkhiyan fortress, Mahmud Khan fell, and Tashkent was captured |
| 1504 | Hissar was captured, followed by Kunduz, Talikan, Badakhshan, and Balkh |
| 1505 | After a ten-month siege, Urgench was captured |
| 1507 | Herat is taken |
| 1509 | The Kazakh sultans were defeated; Signak, Yassi, Sabran, then Marv, Mashhad, and Tus were captured |

As a result, most of Dasht-i Qipchaq, Khwarazm, Movarounnahr, and Khurasan were united under the Shaybanids. Shaybani Khan ordered the restoration of the mausoleum of Khoja Ahmad Yasawi in Turkestan, which testifies to his special attention to religious authority.

### Battle of Marw (1510)

Shaybani Khan, calling himself "Khalif ur-Rahman and Imam az-Zaman," expressed his desire to become the religious leader of the entire Muslim world and planned a campaign against Shiite Iran. This caused sharp resistance from Shah Ismail, the founder of the Safavid state. In the autumn of 1510, at the battle of Marv, Shaybani Khan's small army was defeated, and he himself perished. As Ibn Ruzbehan writes, Shaybani Khan recalled the main part of his army in 1509 to maintain stability in Maveraunnahr and prevent Kazakh attacks; this circumstance had a serious impact on the outcome of the battle.

### Battle of Gijduvan (1512)

Shah Ismail proposed an alliance to Babur. In the autumn of 1511, Babur, with the help of Iranian troops, captured Hisar, Kölob, Kunduz, Badakhshan, and Qarshi, and took Samarkand for the third time. However, the looting of the Iranian Kyzylbash (especially in Samarkand and Karshi), the return of Babur under a foreign flag, and his political adherence to the Shia madhhab intensified the dissatisfaction of the population. On April 28, 1512, at Chuli Malik, Ubaydullah Sultan and Timur Sultan forced Babur to retreat to Hisar. On November 24, 1512, in a decisive battle near Gijduvan, Ubaydullah Sultan defeated the allied forces, and the Iranian commander Najmi Sani was killed.

After this, Babur finally left Maveraunnahr, established himself in Kabul, and in 1526, in the battle of Panipat, defeated the Delhi Sultan Ibrahim Lodi, laying the foundation for the Baburid Empire in India (1526-1858).

## 3. Struggle between Ubaydulla Khan I and the 16th century

After Shaybani Khan's death, central power weakened, and the beks and sultans were nominally subordinate to the khans. His uncle Kuchkunchi Khan (1512–1530) ascended the throne, followed by Abu Said (1530–1533). During this period, Khorezm emerged as an independent state.

### Ubaydulla Khan I (1533-1540)

Ubaydullah Sultan, ruler of Bukhara since 1512, was elected as the supreme ruler of the entire state in 1533. He moved the capital from Samarkand to Bukhara, which is why the state became known as the Bukhara Khanate. After a five-month siege, he captured Herat, reunited Maveraunnahr and Khurasan, put an end to Iranian pressure, and put an end to internecine strife. Ubaydulla Khan built the Mir-i-Arab Madrasah in Bukhara, the Miraq Said Ghiyas Garden, and a bridge over the Zarafshon River. He wrote poetry in Arabic, Persian, and Uzbek under the pseudonyms "Ubaydi" and "Kul Ubaydi."

### The era of dual power

In the 16th century, the struggle for Bukhara and Samarkand intensified. After the short reign of Abdulla Khan I (1540–1541), a dual power emerged: Abdullatif Khan in Samarkand and Abdulaziz Khan in Bukhara, who considered himself a khan. Subsequently, power passed to the son of Suyunchi Khan, the ruler of Tashkent, Nauryz Ahmad Khan (Barak Khan, 1551–1556). He established friendly relations with the Ottomans, invited Turkish Janissaries to his service, built a madrasah in Tashkent, and landscaped the area around the Dargom canal in Samarkand. In 1556, the ruler of Balkh, Pirmuhammad Khan (1556–1561), ascended the throne.

## 4. Abdulla Khan II and the restoration of a centralized state

### To come to power

Abdullah Sultan, supported by the Jubar Sheikh Muhammad Islam (1493–1563), began ruling in Bukhara in 1557. In 1561, he broke with Pirmuhammad and proclaimed his father, Iskandar Sultan (1561–1583), as khan, but in reality, power remained in the hands of Abdulla Khan. After his father's death in 1583, he officially became a khan under the name Abdullah Khan II.

### Unification wars

Abdulla Khan II was opposed by five sons of Nauryz Ahmad Khan—the rulers of Tashkent, Turkestan, Sayram, Akhsikent, and Andijan—with the help of Kazakh sultans. As a result of continuous wars, the following territories were united:

| Year | Territory |
|---|---|
| 1573 | Fergana |
| 1574 | Shahrisabz, Karshi, Hisar |
| 1578 | Samarkand |
| 1582 | Tashkent, Shohruhiya, Sayram, Ohangaron |
| 1583 | Balkh |
| 1584 | Badakhshan |
| 1588 | Herat (after a nine-month siege) |
| 1595 | Khorezm (third move) |

Thus, Abdulla Khan II conquered all of Mawarannahr, Khorasan, Khwarazm, and Turkestan, and restored the state established by Shaybani Khan. These wars also had a difficult side: in the Tashkent oasis, small cities such as Faroqat, Chinaz, and Shahruhiya were destroyed, and the Marv Dam was destroyed by order of the khan; According to Abulgazi, the residents of Khorezm left their homes due to heavy taxes. Despite this, the majority of the population—merchants, artisans, aristocrats, and clergy—who were tired of fragmentation, supported the idea of a centralized state.

### Foreign policy

In foreign policy, Abdulla Khan II raised the issue of cooperation with the Ottomans against Iran in correspondence. Relations were established with India: the first ambassador, Khoja Altymish, was sent to the Baburid Akbarshah in 1572, and embassies were exchanged in 1577, 1586, and 1590, strengthening trade relations. Trade with Russia grew, and in 1574, the embassy of Zachary Bogdanov arrived. But the alliance's plan against Iran failed, as each country had its own geopolitical interests. In recent years, the Kazakh Sultan Tavakkal attacked Tashkent and Samarkand, and in Iran, Shah Abbas I began to oust the Shaybanids from Khorasan.

### The end of the dynasty

In February 1598, Abdulla Khan II died. His only son, Abdulmumin Khan, was killed in a conspiracy that same year due to the cruelty and extermination of his relatives. The ascendant to the throne, Pirmuhammad II (1598-1601), was killed by the Ashtarkhanid Bakimuhammad in 1601 in Bagishamal, near Samarkand. With this, the Shaybanid dynasty, which had ruled for a century, came to an end.

## 5. State administration

In the political system of the khanate, alongside the traditions of state governance formed from the Samanids to the Timurids, there were also traditions inherent to nomads. The khan possessed unlimited power, and the rules of sharia were strictly observed in governance. Initially, supreme power passed to the eldest representative of the dynasty; Starting from the 40s of the 16th century, the transfer of power from father to son intensifies, and under Abdullah Khan II, it takes on a clear form. Administratively, the khanate was divided into provinces and tumans.

The highest state institution was called the dargah. This chancellery, headed by a prime minister and led by the khan, considered political, financial, and military matters, and its decisions only came into force after being approved by the khan.

### The main positions in the department were:

Divanbegi (Prime Minister) — managed financial and economic affairs;

The naqib was the khan's first advisor; at receptions, he sat in the first place to the left of the khan, and his name was mentioned first in decrees; he was entrusted with military and diplomatic duties; he was appointed mainly from the house of the seyyids;

Atalik — the educator of a minor prince, who actually governed the region allocated to the prince;

- Kukaldash ("one mother's suckler") — collected information about friends and enemies of the state, studied the attitude of citizens to politics;
- Yasaul — organized events between the khan and the princes;
- Kushbegi (Amir of Hunting) — organized the Khan's hunting;
- Eshikogaboshi — responsible for security and internal order in the residence;
- Mirohur, shigaul (reception of ambassadors), chukhrabashi, kurchibashi, and others.

Above religious positions stood the sheikh-ul-islam, sadr, qazi-kalon, and mufti. Although Shaybani Khan weakened the political influence of religious leaders, from the middle of the 16th century, the sheikhs of Juybar rose to the level of deciding the fate of the supreme ruler, and the position of sheikh-ul-islam was also appointed from their house. This situation indicates the intensification of interaction between the khan's power and the religious elite.

## 6. Military Affairs

The military system of the Shaybanids was based on the structure of the armies of Chingis Khan and Amir Temur, and changes characteristic of Dasht-i Qipchaq traditions were introduced. The khan himself was considered the supreme commander-in-chief. The army was divided into tumans (ten thousand), thousands, hundreds, and tens.

### Army structure

In the combat ranks, the army was divided into the following parts: in front in the form of a semicircle — manglay (avant-garde) and in front — fast forward; in the center — kalb; on the right wing — barongor, on the left wing — javongor; in the rear — hirovul (arergard). A detachment of 20-30 thousand people standing in reserve under the banner was called a tugchi. The army included the subdivisions of Zabangir (tongue-holding) and Khabargir (reconnaissance), as well as the auxiliary subdivision of Chanoh, which trailed behind.

### Weapons and military ranks

The main weapons were bows, spears, swords, clubs, and tabarzins, while protective equipment included shields, armor, and jibas. To prevent the horses from being hit by bullets, a blanket was thrown over them. The troops included the Naftandoz and Manjaniqchi units. During the reign of Abdullah Khan II, matchlock rifles and cannons were imported from the Ottoman Empire and Russia, the position of artilleryman appeared, and Barak Khan hired 300 Turkish Janissaries for his service. Among the military positions were the tavochi (who handled the gathering of troops, payment of salaries, and stopping places), the jevachi (arms supply), the kurchi (king's guards), the yalovbardor (banner bearer), the qazi askar, and the mufti askar (military judges).

During the campaign, food was confiscated from the population, which was called a yurt-aul. Babur praised the Shaybanid tactic of "tolgama," that is, bypassing the enemy flanks and attacking from the rear. This method was based on the mobility of nomadic cavalry troops.

## 7. Land ownership and tax system

### Types of land ownership

During the Shaybanid era, there were four main types of land ownership. The mulks of the sultan (mamlaka) were state lands, and their sale, gifting, or transfer to waqf were under the sole jurisdiction of the khan. Mulki Halis (Hurri Halis) are private lands provided to individuals for their services. Waqf lands were provided to religious institutions. The lands of rural communities were at the disposal of the community.

The forms of land ownership provided in exchange for service were called suyurgal (transmitted from generation to generation, exempt from taxes; fully privileged — darubast suyurgal), tanho, tiul, ikto, and tarkhanlik. Abdulla Khan II gave all the regions to his pires, the Jubar Khojas, as a darubast. The Khojas of Juybar owned large lands, pastures, shops, mills, and caravanserais in Bukhara, Samarkand, Nasaf, and Marv, and influenced the appointment of officials.

To develop agriculture, abandoned and rainfed lands were transferred to state ownership, and those who worked on them were provided with assistance and exempted from taxes for a certain period.

### Taxes

According to sources, more than forty types of duties, taxes, and obligatory payments existed during the Shaybanid era. The population that paid taxes was called citizens (raiyat).

| Tax or liability | Content |
|---|---|
| Kharaj | Land tax — 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, sometimes 1/2 of the harvest |
| Ushr | 1/10 of the harvest from the owned lands |
| Avarisot | Wartime emergency tax |
| Mark | Craftsmen's and shopkeepers' tax |
| Foundation | Rented store payment |
| Tagar | Gathering food for the army |
| Manure | Redemption of livestock and food from nomads |
| Mardikor, fortress, begor | Obligations for the construction of canals, fortresses, and walls |

The lack of separation between the khan's treasury and the state treasury, the arbitrariness of officials, and the practice of leasing taxes led to chaos in the tax system.

## 8. Monetary reforms, crafts, and trade

### Monetary reforms

The basis of money circulation in the 16th century was the silver coin ("khani") and the copper dinar; the smallest copper coin was called "pul" or "black coin." Gold coins were minted only during the reigns of Shaybani Khan and Abdullah Khan II. Three major monetary reforms were carried out under the Shaybanids.

Shaybani Khan's first reform (1507–1508) was announced at the Herat Cathedral Mosque. The weight of the new silver coin was 5.2 grams (the Temurid coin was 4.8 grams), and in 1508, identical coins were minted in Samarkand, Bukhara, Marv, Herat, Mashhad, and other cities, putting an end to the depreciation of money. The second was the Kuchkunchi Khan reform. The wars after 1510, the harsh winter of 1512-1513, and the excess of copper coins led to a crisis. As a result of the reform, which lasted for fifteen years, identical coins began to be minted throughout the state starting from 1525. The third reform of Abdulla Khan II was carried out in stages, starting from the 60s of the 16th century: gold coins were constantly minted in Bukhara, silver minting was centralized in Bukhara from 1583, and copper coins were issued for daily trade. This reform put an end to the monetary crisis and stopped the outflow of silver.

### Craft

In Bukhara and Samarkand, there were more than 130 types of crafts. Bukhara alacha, zandanachi fabric, and Samarkand cloth (red variety) were also known abroad. As Babur wrote, the best paper in the world is produced in Samarkand; paper mills were located in Siyahab, and "Miri Ibrahimi" paper was famous. Metalworking, jewelry making, pottery (Humdanak in Samarkand, Guzar Kosagaron in Bukhara), and ganch carving were developed; On the instructions of Abdulla Khan II, seven cannons were cast.

### Trade

More than a hundred types of products were sold in the markets of Bukhara and Samarkand; the markets were specialized and were called "chorsu," "rasta," "tim," and "tok." The bazaar was not only a trading center but also a cultural center where government decrees were announced and ghazals were recited. Trade routes connected Central Asia with Afghanistan, China, India, Iran, Siberia, and the Caspian region. Cotton fabrics and leather goods were exported to Russia, fabrics to Siberia, and fur, wax, and honey from there. Later, the shift of trade routes to the sea had a negative impact on these routes.

## 9. Cultural life

### Literature

In the 16th century, writers such as Muhammad Salih, Khoja Afsodi, Vasifi, Majlisi, Ubaydi, Mushfiqi, and Nisori worked fruitfully. In Hasanhoji Nisori's work "Muzakkiri Ahbob" ("Memories of Friends"), information is provided about 228 poets. The rulers themselves were also creative: Shaybani Khan compiled poetic divans and engaged in calligraphy, Ubaydulla Khan I created divans in three languages (the Uzbek divan consists of 306 ghazals and 435 rubais), and Abdulla Khan II wrote poems in Uzbek and Persian under the pseudonym "Khan."

### Historiography and Science

During this period, the "Zubdat al-Asar," "Fath-name," two "Shaybani-names," the "Mehman-name of Bukhara," Vasifi's "Badoe' ul-Vake'ah," and the "Abdulla-name" were created. By order of Kuchkunchi Khan, Sharafiddin Ali Yazdi's "Zafarnoma" was translated into Uzbek. Important sources include "Tarikh-i Rashidi" by Mirzo Muhammad Haidar and "Tarikh-i Jadidai Tashkent" by Hafiz Kuhaki, the grandson of Ali Qushchi. In medicine, the works of Sultan Ali "Dastur al-Ilaj" and Ubaydullah Kahhal "Cure of Diseases" (1562–1567) were created.

### Formation

Education began at the age of six: after two years of schooling, they transitioned to madrasas. The madrasah consisted of three stages, each lasting seven years, totaling twenty-one years. Theology, arithmetic, jurisprudence, geometry, logic, music, and poetry were taught.

### Architecture and Religious Life

During the reign of Abdulla Khan II, more than a hundred structures were built. The Mir Arab, Abdulla Khan, and Modari Khan madrasas were built in Bukhara, the city wall was repaired, the Khaniya and Kulbaba Kukeldash madrasas and the watershed were built in Samarkand, the Barak Khan and Kukeldash madrasas were built in Tashkent, the Shaybani Khan mosque was built in Turkestan, and the Kasym Sheikh mosque was repaired in Karmana. The English traveler Anthony Jenkinson described Bukhara as "a large city with brick buildings and magnificent buildings" and particularly noted that the baths were built with great skill.

In religious life, the leaders of the Naqshbandi order—the Sheikhs of Jubar (Khoja Muhammad Islam, Khoja Saad, and others) and Mir Arab from Yemen—had great influence.

## Key concepts

- The Shaybanids were a dynasty descended from Shayban, the son of Jochi Khan (1500–1601).
- Dashti Qipchaq is a steppe region north of the Syr Darya, the homeland of the nomadic Uzbeks.
- The Kyzylbash were the main military force of the Safavids, warriors clad in a turban with 12 red stripes.
- The dargah is the highest state institution headed by the khan.
- Naqib, atalyk, and kokeldash were the highest court offices in the khanate.
- Sheikhs of Juybar are influential Naqshbandi sheikhs from the village of Juybar near Bukhara.
- Suyurgal, darubast—hereditary, tax-exempt landed property.
- Kharaj, ushr, and tamga are the main types of taxes.
- Tolgama is a method of attacking from the rear, bypassing the opponent's wing.
