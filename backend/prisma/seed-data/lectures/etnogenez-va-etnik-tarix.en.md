## 1. Theoretical foundations of studying ethnogenesis

Ethnogenesis is the history of the origin of any people. Ethnic history, on the other hand, studies the further development of a formed people. In various years, A.Yu. Yakubovsky, S.P. Tolstov, L.V. Oshanin, A.N. Bernshtam, T.K. Khodjaev, I.M. Jabbarov, K.Sh. Shoniyozov, and A.A. Askarov conducted research on the origin of the peoples of Central Asia, including the Uzbek people. Some observations regarding the similarity of appearance, customs, and languages can be found among authors and travelers of works written in ancient Arabic, Iranian, and Turkic languages. However, ancient and medieval authors failed to reveal the essence of these processes. Research conducted in the 19th–20th centuries also failed to fully reveal the reasons for the similarity in the origin, culture, and languages of peoples. The textbook emphasizes that compared to other branches of history (archaeology, ethnography, anthropology), the ethnogenesis of the Uzbek people still requires serious research.

### Principles of Studying Ethnogenesis

Summarizing the results of archaeological, anthropological, ethnographic, and linguistic research, scientists proposed focusing on several principles. Firstly, the origin of a people is a multifaceted process that encompasses the culture and social structure, biological characteristics, place in society, language, and self-awareness of that people. Secondly, the issue of ethnogenesis cannot be simplified one-sidedly: all components that are significant in the origin of a people must be taken into account. Thirdly, since ethnogenesis is a complex and long-term process, it is advisable to restore it gradually.

In the process of ethnogenesis, the emergence of new traditions as a result of the ethno-cultural synthesis (fusion, mixing) of the traditions of the indigenous — autochthonous — population with groups of arriving residents deserves special attention.

### Ethnic factors and ethnic characteristics

According to the theory recognized in ethnology, ethnic factors include territorial, economic-cultural, anthropological-type, and political unity (organization within the state). Ethnic characteristics consist of the unity of language, ethnic name (ethnonym), and self-awareness, i.e., the commonality of historical destiny. These factors and signs do not form simultaneously, but gradually over a long historical process. After the formation of their main part, a nation (people) is formed.

## 2. Basic ethnic concepts

The science of peoples was founded approximately 2.5 thousand years ago in Ancient Greece, which is why Greek words are often encountered in this science. Among the ancient Greeks, one of the words meaning "people" is "demos" (the main part of the population; "democracy" — "people's power"), and the second was "ethnos" — its exact translation means "people."

### Ethnic community and ethnos

Ethnic community — a social and ethnic association that emerged within a specific social structure during the process of natural-historical development. Its synonym is ethnos. The word "people" can be used both in a broad sense (for example, "people of Uzbekistan," "peoples of the world"), and in a narrow sense (for example, "Uzbek people," "Kazakh people"), and even applied to a small group ("mahalla people," "village people"); "ethnic community" is a more specific concept. An ethnos can be briefly characterized as an organized linguistic and cultural community.

In science, three stages of ethnic community are distinguished. A tribe is an ethnic community characteristic of the last stage of the primitive communal system, which is distinguished by its division into clans, its language (dialect), territory, name (ethnonym), and traditions to which its members are subject; with the emergence of statehood, the tribal union loses its significance, but some features remain. Elat (people) is a term used among the Turkic-speaking and Persian-speaking population in relation to a tribe, tribal union, state, or population of a specific territory; later, the term "people" came to be used instead of "el" or "elat" in relation to ethnic units leading a sedentary lifestyle. The nation is the highest stage of ethnic unity.

### Ethnographic and ethnic group

An ethnographic group is an integral part of a specific ethnos and is distinguished by its dialect, economic activities, and certain aspects of its lifestyle. Usually, this is how a part of a tribe or people is called that has migrated to another area and has not yet fully integrated into the composition of another people; for example, the Kipchaks, Kurams, and generally the nomadic Turkic tribes were ethnographic groups. An ethnic group arises as a result of the disintegration of a specific people; it lives for a long time within another people, adopts its language, culture, and customs, and calls itself by the name of that people.

### Ethnic processes and traditions

The continuous development of ethnic, economic, and cultural ties between groups within an ethnic community is called ethnic processes. They are divided into processes prone to separation and merger: separation is more characteristic of nomadic pastoralists, while merger is more characteristic of sedentary populations. Cultural features that each nation assimilates during its historical and cultural development and transmits from generation to generation are called ethnic traditions. They are formed in connection with the socio-economic and natural-geographical conditions of the people. According to researchers, kinship communities gradually transform into tribal communities. A clan is a union of relatives living together and working in cooperation, which is of great importance as one of the first stages of the ethnic formation of peoples.

## 3. Ancient migrations and ethno-cultural processes

### Internal and external migration

The territory of Central Asia was developed by humans as early as the ancient Stone Age. In the Late Paleolithic and Mesolithic, tribal communities were located in mountainous and foothill areas, along the banks of rivers and lakes. Based on the requirements of hunting, fishing, and gathering farming, as the development of lands rich in food and raw materials for stone tools expanded, the migration of the population from one territory to another developed. Internal migrations are movements within a common region that determine the expansion of the territorial borders of the indigenous population; external migrations are the movements of population groups arriving from distant countries.

With the emergence of productive farming and animal husbandry, the process of searching for water sources, fertile lands, and pastures accelerated. The distribution of the population took on a mixed character, and kinship ties lost their former significance. Based on a group of tribes densely settled in a specific territory, ethnic communities—ancient peoples—formed; in the development of their linguistic, cultural, economic, and political ties, territory plays a primary role.

### Two theories

There are two theories in science regarding the relationships between indigenous (autochthonous) and foreign peoples. The first recognizes the predominantly autochthonous, gradual development of society; the second shows the development of peoples in connection with autochthonous development and migration. From historical ethnography, it is known that "closed" societies that developed without external influence, based solely on autochthonous traditions, were culturally backward. Peoples who followed the path of interaction between autochthonous traditions and migrations have achieved cultural, economic, and technological successes since ancient times.

### Migrations in Central Asia

According to archaeological data, migration processes intensified particularly during the Eneolithic and Bronze Ages. During this period, the widespread settlement of ancient farmers is noted in the Kopetdag foothills, the foothills of the Murghab River, Northern Afghanistan, and the Zarafshan oasis. BC. In the middle of the 2nd millennium BC, the migration of the pastoralist population from the steppes of Eurasia began. The causes of these processes arose under the influence of environmental, economic, and social factors. According to the textbook's conclusion, the ethnic history of Central Asia is characterized by migrations, processes of assimilation (mixing) between tribes on various economic grounds, and long-term cultural-economic cooperation.

### Origin of the people's name

In ethnology, there are two possibilities that an ancient people gave itself a name: the name may be associated with the name of the territory where the people lived (toponym), or it may reflect the ethnic character of the people (ethnonym). An important point is that the self-designation of a people occurs much later than the beginning of the ethnogenesis process.

### Location of indigenous population

The textbook emphasizes an important conclusion: at the time of the arrival of steppe herders, numerous indigenous settled populations engaged in agriculture lived in various regions of Central Asia. They were distinguished by highly developed material culture, craftsmanship, construction, and architecture. It is not right to leave this population at a distance from their ancestors. Cattle-breeding groups that penetrated agricultural oases gradually dissolved into the local population and adopted its culture. This process may have initially proceeded peacefully, but military clashes are not ruled out—the ancient parts of the Avesta recount tribal disputes, wars, and invasions.

The language and culture of the indigenous Bronze Age population differed from the language and culture of the newcomers, so bilingualism persisted in some regions for some time, and then the language and beliefs of the newcomers became widespread. According to the first written sources, b.c. In the first half of the 1st millennium BC, the Parthian, Bactrian, Sogdian, and Khwarazmian languages were widespread in sedentary agricultural oases; over time, they were displaced by the Turkic and Tajik languages, and this process also occurred in stages under conditions of bilingualism.

### Textbook Summary

Ancient ethnogenesis and ethnic history developed in the following order:

> indigenous (autochthonous) traditions → synthesis (mixing, joining) → renewal → new traditions and ethno-cultural innovations.

Therefore, the theses that the modern people are the direct descendants of any individual tribe or that their ethnogenesis began with the migration of Aryans (Iranians, Turks, "proto-Turks") are assessed in the textbook as dubious: such views are an attempt to attribute the origin of the modern people to "great ethnoi." The Aryans who arrived in Central Asia also gradually merged with the ancient local population, and on this basis, ancient peoples known from the first written sources—the ancestors of the region's modern peoples—formed. The textbook expresses another important thought: addressing the historical and cultural heritage of peoples, especially the problem of ethnogenesis, must be free from competition and serve regional harmony and good neighborliness.

## 5. The issue of the formation of the Uzbek people

### History of scientific views

In the mid-1930s, the task of writing the history of Central Asian peoples was set, archaeological and ethnographic expeditions were organized, and the analysis of ethnogenesis processes became a separate task.

However, as noted in the textbook, the question of the significance of the indigenous population living in the region prior to the Bronze Age in ethnogenesis remains open. In some publications of the early 21st century, the identification of the beginning of the ethnogenesis of modern peoples with nomadic pastoralist tribes such as "Aryan-Iranians," "Aryan-Turks," "Proto-Turks," and "Bronze Age Oghuzes" has not been sufficiently scientifically substantiated

### Racial-anthropological aspect

Until recently, there was no consensus in science regarding the formation period of the Central Asian interfluve race characteristic of the Uzbek people: some scholars believed it to have formed 6-8 thousand years ago, others 3 thousand years ago, and a third group believed it to have formed in the 6th century. Subsequent anthropological studies confirmed that its formation began much earlier. Migrations during the era of the Ahamenids, Alexander the Great, the Yuezhi Kushans, the Turkic Khaganate, the Arab Caliphate, and the Mongols influenced the culture, language, and lifestyle of the peoples of Central Asia. However, their influence on the racial and ethnic history of the indigenous peoples was insignificant: the newcomers merged with the numerically predominant indigenous population. According to the research of anthropologist T.K. Khodjaev, in all historical eras, the local population has always been taller than the newcomers.

### Main stages

In the Early Iron Age, the sedentary population living in ancient agricultural oases began to be called by regional names: Sogdians in Sogdiana, Khwarazmians in Khwarazm, and Bactrians in Bactria. In written sources, the nomadic Saka and Massagetae are also mentioned. According to the language of the "Avesto" and Khwarazmian, Sogdian, and Bactrian inscriptions, the settled population consisted of peoples speaking the Eastern Iranian language.

During the Achaemenid era (c. The second half of the 6th–4th centuries BC) did not change in terms of language and culture. It is possible that the communities of the Syr Darya basin and the Fergana Valley were Turkic-speaking; It is possible that this language was spoken by the tribes that created the Chirikrabat and Babishmulla cultures in the Lower Syr Darya, and later by the population of Kangju. Attention should be paid to the close neighborliness and ties between Eastern Iranian-speaking peoples and Turkic-speaking tribes. In the 4th–6th centuries BC, Yuezhi, Chionites, Kidarites, and Hephthalites participated in the ethnic formation; From the end of the 4th century, the sedentariness of the Chionites and Hephthalites intensifies, and by the 5th–6th centuries, their main part transitions to agriculture alongside livestock farming.

During the period of the Turkic Khaganate (VI-VIII centuries), the penetration of Turkic-speaking groups into the Tashkent oasis, Zeravshan, Kashkadarya, Surkhandarya, Fergana, and Khorezm intensified, and many of them became sedentary; the bilingualism of the Eastern Iranian and Turkic-speaking population continued. During the Arab conquest (VIII century), the local population remained ethnically unchanged: some Arab tribes established settlements in the oases of Qashqadaryo and Zarafshon, occupied separate guzares in Marv and Bukhara, and the Arabic language spread as the Islamic religion and literary language.

During the Karluk and Karakhanid era (second half of the 8th century — 12th century), the Karluk state was formed in the middle of the 9th century, covering the Semirechye, the Chuy Valley, the Tashkent oasis, and northern Fergana; Since the middle of the 10th century, it has been referred to as the Karakhanid state. According to many researchers, the Karluk people played a major role in the formation of the Uzbek people. Alongside the Karluk, the Chigil, Yagmo, and Khalachi also lived. At the beginning of the 11th century, the southern territories passed into the hands of the Karakhanids, and Turkic strata existed in all territories of Mawarannahr.

Mahmud Koshgari called the Turkic dialect, which was widespread in the Karakhanid state, the "most open" language. In this language, epics such as Ahmad Yugnaki's "Hibat ul-Haqayiq," Yusuf Hos Hajib's "Kutadgu Bilig," Ahmad Yassawi's "Hikmatlar," as well as "Oghuznoma," "Alpomish," and "Gorogly" were created in the 9th–12th centuries. From the 10th century, the Persian-speaking inhabitants of Mawarannahr and Khurasan began to call themselves "Tajiks." The dynasties of the Ghaznavids, Seljuks, and Khwarazmshahs-Anushteginiids also belonged to the Turkic tribe. In the 10th–11th centuries, Turkic and Persian-Tajik languages completely replaced Eastern Iranian languages (Khorezmian, Sogdian). During the Mongol period (XIII-XIV centuries), Turkic and Mongol tribes such as the Jalayir, Barlas, Ming, Mangyt, and Kungrat migrated to the Tashkent oasis, Fergana, Kashkadarya, and Surkhandarya. By the mid-14th century, the Mongol tribes had become Turkic.

### Textbook Summary

According to modern scientific views, the Uzbek nation was formed in the 10th century as a result of the mixing of Eastern Iranian and Turkic-speaking populations (Khwarazmians, Sogdians, Bactrians, and Turkic tribes); In the 11th–12th centuries, its development took place based on Turkic components. The core of the Uzbek people consisted of local farming tribes that formed in ancient times on the territory of modern Uzbekistan, as well as nomadic pastoralist tribes that mixed with them. According to research, the Uzbek people consist of two ancient ethnic strata: Eastern Iranian and Turkic-speaking. Although language is an important aspect of ethnogenesis, attention should also be paid to anthropological and genetic features. For example, Turkic-speaking Kazakhs and Kyrgyz belong to the Mongoloid race, while Uzbeks and Turkmens are mainly representatives of the European race.

## 6. The origin of the ethnonym "Uzbek."

There are various views on the origin of the ethnonym "Uzbek." A group of researchers associates it with the name of the Golden Horde khan Uzbekkhan (1312–1341). Others reject this opinion, believing that the name "Uzbek" belonged to Turkic tribes that lived in the Ak-Orda region, Western Siberia, and part of Kazakhstan even before Uzbek Khan (the khan's name may have been taken from the name of a tribe). Some researchers believe that the term originates from the meanings of "the most bek," "free," "independent," and began to be applied to our people in the 12th–14th centuries.

It cannot be said that the Uzbek, Turkmen, Kazakh, and Karakalpak peoples appeared at the same time. These peoples have lived in their current territories for many centuries, but in distant times they did not bear modern names — common "Turkic" or tribal. The Turkic population of Dasht-i Qipchaq came to Maveraunnahr in the late 15th and early 16th centuries, mixed with the indigenous Turkic population, and the name of the population was "Uzbek." However, the textbook emphasizes that the tribes who came from Dasht-i Qipchaq did not exert a significant influence on the ethnogenesis, customs, and cultural traditions of the local population; on the contrary, they were introduced into the environment of the local population under the influence of a high culture with deep historical roots.

## 7. The modern significance of studying ethnic history

### Requirements of scientific approach

Issues of ethnogenesis and ethnic history today hold not only scientific but also socio-cultural significance. Concepts regarding the origin of a people are directly linked to their self-awareness, historical memory, and national pride. Therefore, in this matter, scientific objectivity, reliance on sources, and the rejection of one-sided conclusions acquire particular significance. In studying ethnogenesis, the most effective method is a comprehensive approach—a joint analysis of data from archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, ethnography, and written sources, as each individual source reveals only one facet of a people's history.

### Multi-ethnic society and historical memory

The history of Central Asia is a history of long-term interaction, intermingling, and cooperation between various peoples and cultures. Therefore, when addressing ethnic history, the idea of mutual respect, cooperation, and awareness of a common historical heritage should be the leading one, rather than the competition between peoples for superiority or "ancient age." Uzbeks, Tajiks, Karakalpaks, Kazakhs, Turkmens, Kyrgyz, and other peoples live side by side in the region, forming a common economic and cultural space. This process also allows for the use of historical knowledge in school education as a means of fostering a sense of tolerance, respect for the culture of other peoples, and patriotism in young people.

## Conclusion

Ethnogenesis is a multifaceted, long-term process that cannot be explained solely by the data of one science or the history of any tribe. The ethnic history of Central Asia was formed as a result of the interaction, mixing, and renewal of the traditions of the indigenous sedentary population and migrations during different periods. According to the scientific point of view of the textbook, the Uzbek people were formed in the 10th century on the basis of the Eastern Iranian and Turkic-speaking layers and developed in the 11th-12th centuries on the basis of the Turkic component; The term "Uzbek" began to be used later—at the end of the 15th and beginning of the 16th centuries—in relation to the entire population.

## Key concepts

- **Ethnogenesis** — the history of a people's origin; ethnic history - the further development of the people.
- **Ethnos** — an organized linguistic and cultural community; ethnic community - tribe, people, nation.
- **Autochthonous population** — indigenous population; migration - population movement.
- **Ethnocultural synthesis (assimilation)** — the mixing of population groups.
- **Ethnic factors** — territorial, economic-cultural, anthropological, and political community; ethnic characteristics - language, ethnonym, self-awareness.
- **Ethnographic group, ethnic group** — units within an ethnos.
- **Ethnonym** — the name of a people; toponym - place name.
- The Central Asian Two-River Race is an anthropological type characteristic of the Uzbek people.
