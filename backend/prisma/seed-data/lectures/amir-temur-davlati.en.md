# The rise of Uzbek statehood during the reign of Amir Temur

> The lecture was prepared based on Topic 1 of the "History of Uzbekistan" textbook by B.J. Eshov and A.A. Odilov (Book II, 2020), as well as sources related to the period of Amir Temur.

Purpose of the lecture: to provide students with a holistic understanding of the formation of a centralized state in Transoxiana in the second half of the 14th and early 15th centuries, the administrative, military, and diplomatic systems of Amir Temur's empire, and the cultural life of this period; to develop critical work skills with historical sources.

10. Sources on the History of the Amir Temur Period
11. There are many historical sources regarding the life and activities of the statesman and commander Amir Temur, who left a profound mark on world history. They can be divided into three groups: chronicles written at the court of Amir Temur or by his descendants; Works by authors contemporary with the Sahibkiran but unrelated to his court; Diaries of European ambassadors and travelers. When working with a source, it is necessary to consider who the author was, who commissioned the work, and when it was written.

### Works of court historians

One of the first sources belonging to this period is the work "Ruznoma-i Ghazavati Hindi" by Ghiyath al-Din Ali Yazdi, written by order of the ruler in 1399-1403. Nizamiddin Shami's "Zafarnama" encompasses the events that began with Timur's instruction in 1401 and continued until the end of 1404. Sharafiddin Ali Yazdi's "Zafarnama" was completed in 1425 by order of Sultan Ibrahim Mirza; V.V. Bartold characterized it as a work that influenced subsequent historians, and Alisher Navoi included it among the perfect works of a high style.

The works of Hafizi Abru "Zubdat at-tavorihi Boysunguri" and Abdurazzak Samarkandi's "Matlai sadain wa majmai bahrayn" are also important sources for the history of the Timurid period.

### Contemporaries outside the court

The famous Arab historian Ibn Khaldun highly praised the potential of the group that arrived to hand over the city keys during the capture of Damascus by Amir Temur. Ibn Arabshah (1389-1450), a native of Damascus, came to Samarkand with his family at the age of 12 and lived here from 1401 to 1408; his work "Ajoyib al-Makdur fi Akhbori Taymur" contains many information about the state of Amir Temur. Ibn Arabshah often had a negative attitude toward Amir Temur, but he recognized his qualities such as decisiveness and courage. Considering the author's attitude is an important condition for the critical reading of a source.

### Witnesses from Europe

One of the valuable sources is the work of the ambassador of the King of Castile and Leon Henry III, Rui González de Clavijo, "Diary of a journey to Samarkand — the palace of Amir Temur (1403-1406)." A group of 13 people arrived in Samarkand at the end of August 1404, were received by the ruler on September 8, and returned to their homeland on November 21. Clavijo left much information about the state, court receptions, trade, and the life of the people. The Bavarian armorer Johann Schiltberger was captured by the Turkish Sultan Bayazid in the Battle of Nikopol in 1396, and by Amir Temur's troops in the Battle of Ankara in 1402, only returning to his homeland in 1427. "The Tale of the Lame Timur," which appeared in Rus in the 15th century, was created based on oral reports and is semi-legendary in nature; therefore, it should be used as a historical source with caution.

## 2. Formation of a centralized state.

### Political Crisis in the Chigatai Ulus

In the 40s–50s of the 14th century, mutual fragmentation and tyranny reigned within the territory of the Chigatai Ulus. During the reign of the Mongol khan Kazan Khan (1338–1346), oppression intensified even further. The officials, dissatisfied with the Khan, began to flee to the powerful Emir of their time, Kazagan. In the spring of 1346, in the battle of Karshi, Kazan Khan was killed. Since Emir Kazagan was not a Chingizid, he could not claim the throne according to Turkic-Mongolian custom: he placed Chingizid Danishmandchakhan, and then Bayankulikhan, on the throne, effectively retaining power in his hands. In 1358, Kazagan was killed by conspirators. After this, the Chigatai Ulus disintegrated into small principalities: Haji Barlas in Kesh, Bayazid Jalayir in Khujand, Uljay Buga Suldus in Balkh, Amir Kaykhusraw in Khuttalan, and others declared themselves independent rulers. In conditions of severe socio-political and economic crisis, Amir Temur entered the political arena.

### Unification of Maveraunnahr and Khwarazm

From the beginning of the 70s of the 14th century, Amir Temur began a policy of unification: the territories between the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, as well as the Fergana and Shash regions, were included in his jurisdiction. In order to annex Khwarazm peacefully, he sent an ambassador to Husayn Sufi of the Sufi dynasty. Husayn Sufi replied: "I conquered the country with a sword, it can only be taken with a sword," and the second envoy was thrown into prison. As a result, Amir Temur was forced to make five campaigns against Khwarazm (1371, 1373-1374, 1375-1376, 1379 and 1388). In the fifth campaign of 1388, Urgench was captured, the Sufi dynasty was overthrown, and Khwarazm was finally incorporated into the state of Amir Temur.

## 3. The creation of a great empire.

Amir Temur turned his attention to the south in order to unite a single geopolitical territory, end internal conflicts, and create a powerful centralized state. The textbook emphasizes that only a strong empire could put an end to the tyranny and oppression of rulers and promote the development of trade, culture, and creation.

### Khorasan and Iran

The Kurt dynasty ruled in Khorasan. In February 1381, Amir Temur himself traveled to Khorasan, and the keys to Herat were handed over to him; The Sahibkiran ordered the release of 2,000 prisoners. By the mid-80s of the 14th century, all of Khorasan had passed into the hands of Amir Temur. Subsequent campaigns were called "three-year" (1386-1388), "five-year" (1392-1396), and "seven-year" (1399-1405). The textbook notes that Amir Temur did not damage cities and fortresses that surrendered of their own accord, did not allow military personnel into the city, and only sent tax collectors (muhassils). As a result of the "five-year" campaign into Iran, the Muzaffari dynasty was brought to an end.

### The struggle against the Golden Horde

Until 1377, the Ulus of Jochi consisted of two states: the right wing of the Golden Horde and the left wing of the White Horde. When the ruler of Ak-Orda, Urus Khan, attempted to unite the ulus, his opponents were executed; the son of one of the executed, Tokhtamysh Khan, fled to Samarkand to join Amir Temur. Amir Temur assisted him three times and ensured his final conquest of the throne in 1377. However, Tokhtamysh Khan quickly forgot the favor shown to him: in 1385, he attempted to seize Azerbaijan, and at the end of 1388, he sent troops to Movarounnahr. The textbook emphasizes that Amir Temur did not aim to liquidate the Golden Horde: the military actions were aimed at protecting his empire from danger.

On June 18, 1391, on the Kunduzcha plain, Tokhtamysh Khan's army was defeated; in this battle, Amir Temur first applied the method of dividing the army into seven arms (corpses). On April 15, 1395, after a decisive battle on the Terek River, the capital of the Golden Horde, Saroy Berke, was captured. After this blow, the Golden Horde could not recover; this had a positive impact on the liberation of Rus from Mongol rule, and international trade routes returned to the south once again.

### India, Egypt, and the Ottoman Empire

The campaign to India (May 1398 — March 1399) coincides with the period of the "seven-year" war. The captured trophies were sent to Samarkand and Kesh, 120 of which were used in military operations and construction work. By 1400, relations between Amir Temur and the Sultan of Egypt and the Ottoman Sultan Bayazid Yildirim had escalated. Both great powers claimed to dominate the Near and Middle East; Amir Temur's opponents, such as Kara Yusuf and Sultan Ahmad Jalair, were under the protection of Bayazid. Bayazid and Amir Temur exchanged letters four times, but no agreement was reached.

The decisive battle took place on July 20, 1402, on the Chibukabad plain near Ankara. Amir Temur resorted to deception: his grandson Muhammad Sultan was tasked with occupying the strategic height of the Turks, while Sahibkiran's troops surrounded the two flanks of the Turkish army. By evening, the Turks were completely defeated, and the sultan himself was taken prisoner. Amir Timur treated Bayazid well; after his death (1403), he handed over the throne to his son Musa. The textbook emphasizes that the defeat of the Ottomans had a positive significance for European peoples: the Turks' offensive against Europe was postponed for 50 years.

### The Chinese Campaign and the Death of Amir Temur

In July 1404, Amir Temur returned to Samarkand as a victorious ruler and held the wedding of his six grandchildren in September. Due to China's territorial claims to Maveraunnahr, he marched against China with an army on November 27, 1404. That year's winter was one of the harshest in Central Asian history: the water in the Syr Darya froze to one and a half meters. Having caught a cold on the way, Sahibkiran passed away in Otrar on February 18, 1405. For 35 years, Amir Temur conducted military campaigns; his empire included Maveraunnahr, Khorezm, the Caspian regions, modern Afghanistan, Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus, and several territories of Western Asia—totaling 27 countries and regions.

## 4. "Temur's Regulations" is an important source on state administration.

Among the works dedicated to the life and activities of Amir Temur, the "Regulations of Temur" are of particular importance. It is also known by the names "Tuzuki Temuri," "Malmukoti Temuri" ("Temur's Stories"), and "Voqioti Temuri" ("Temur's Adventures"), and is kept in famous libraries around the world.

### The fate of the work

The work was originally written in Old Uzbek (Turkic). Mir Abu Talib Huseyni at-Turbati, who first translated it into Persian, writes that he encountered the work during a pilgrimage and presented the translation to Shah Shah Jahan (1628-1657). The work was published in 1783 in England in English, in 1787 in Paris in French, then translated into Urdu, Russian, Azerbaijani, and Kazakh. Translated into Uzbek in the 19th century in Kokand and Khiva; the modern translation is by Habibullo Karomatov (in a 1989 journal, in a 1991 book). Sharafiddin Ali Yazdi and other Eastern authors note that the work was written by Amir Temur.

### Structure and content

The "Regulations of Temur" consist of two parts. The first part briefly describes Amir Temur's life from the age of seven until his death, his seizure of power in Maveraunnahr, the creation of a centralized state, and his military campaigns. The second part consists of precepts and instructions spoken on behalf of Amir Temur; it covers issues such as whom to rely on in governing the state, the selection of ministers and military commanders, the salaries of sepoys, the order of governing the country, and the rewarding of distinguished individuals. The tradition of correct writing has existed among Turkic-Mongol rulers since ancient times: the "Canon" inherited from the Turkic khagans, and the "Knowledge" of Genghis Khan. Some thoughts in the work have not lost their relevance today:

"Nine parts of the kingdom's affairs I accomplished with counsel, events, and councils, and the remaining part with a sword."

"A strong hand can defeat one, a strong knowledge can defeat a thousand."

## 5. State Management System

During the reign of Amir Temur, the traditions of statehood and the system of governance rose to a high level. He managed to combine local traditions with the best practices of other states of his time.

### Ruler's status

Amir Temur himself was the absolute ruler of the country. However, in accordance with Turkic-Mongolian traditions, he placed Suyurgatmish (1370–1388) on the throne, followed by his son Mahmud Khan, and confined himself to the title of "Amir." By marrying Kazan Khan's daughter, Saray-Mulk-khanum, Chingiz Khan became the son-in-law of the khan's house and received the title "Kuragan." The signature "Temur Kuragon" was placed on official documents and coins, and "Temurbek" on letters and letters. The French scholar L. Keren claimed that the state flag had three rings and the phrase "Strength is in justice."

### Congress and meeting

Since ancient times, the Kurultai has been a large council of Turkic and Mongol tribes convened on the most important issues; the heads of state were elected at the Kurultai and rose on a white felt mat. Amir Temur was declared ruler of Maverannahr by the decision of the kurultai in April 1370 and subsequently maintained this institution, effectively utilizing it: at the 1390 kurultai, the issue of relations with Tokhtamysh Khan was considered, and at the end of 1398, a kurultai was convened before the campaign to India. In the autumn of 1403, when the city of Baylakon was built in Shirvan, a large kurultai was held with the participation of scholars and ulema, where Amir Temur called for the promotion of justice and the rule of law in society. According to Nizamiddin Shami, auditors were sent to the country's regions so that "if any of the oppressed were harmed, they should compensate for the damage from the treasury." After the kurultai, there was a state council: the great council was convened in peace at the palace in Samarkand, and the small council during military campaigns.

### Dorgoh and Divan

The department was divided into two departments. The dargah was the highest state institution, headed by Amir Temur himself; the dargah served as an arzbegi (who delivered complaints to the ruler), a tavaji (who gathered troops by decree), a chief hajib, a treasurer, a jevachi, a bakavulbashi, scribes, and physicians. The ruler of the divan (executive power) was the divanbegi. There were seven ministers in the divan: the minister of the country and the raiyat (population) — the prime minister; the minister of military affairs; the minister of property and taxes; the minister of finance; as well as three border ministers who kept records of taxes, army funds, inherited property, and fines in dependent lands. In the "Regulations," four qualities are required when choosing a minister: nobility and purity of blood; intelligence and insight; awareness of the condition of warriors and subjects and politeness toward them; and patience and peacefulness. Officials who abused their positions were severely punished: Clavijo wrote that in October 1404, two ministers were executed.

### Ulus and local administration

Amir Temur divided the state into four uluses and left them sons and grandchildren: to Jahangir Mirza, the lands of Kabul, Ghazni, and Kandahar were given (after his death, to his son Pirmuhammad), to Umar Sheikh Mirza, first Fergana and then Fars, to Miranshah Mirza, Western Iraq and Azerbaijan, and to Shah Rukh Mirza, Khorasan with its center in Herat. Each region, city, and district had its own ruler, financial divan, judge, mufti, and muhtasib; a kutvol (commandant) was appointed in the fortresses, and a doruğa in the cities and districts. The self-government of mahalla and community elders was also of great importance.

### Twelve Categories and the Judicial System

Amir Temur divided the population into 12 categories and wrote in the "Regulations" that he considered all of them his support: "I compiled the laws of my empire, linking them to these twelve categories." Among them were sayyids and ulema, advisors, emirs and commanders, warriors and subjects, ministers and scribes, doctors, astrologers and engineers, artisans, merchants, and travelers. Ethnic, religious, and linguistic discrimination is not permitted in the state. In the state, more attention is paid to preventing crimes than to punishing them. The courts were specialized, with an army judge, a sharia judge, and a civil judge; the peace was maintained by the darughas, kutvals, and muhtasibs.

## 6. The military sphere and the growth of military skill.

### State security

Amir Temur attached great importance to the foresight of internal and external dangers. The "Regulations of Temur" mandated that correspondents be appointed in every region, city, and military headquarters to record daily events, and a system of messengers was established to promptly notify of external threats. The court and the divan were guarded by twelve thousand warriors.

### Army structure

Amir Temur reorganized the military system left over from the time of Genghis Khan. The army was divided into tens, hundreds, thousands, and ten thousand (tumans). At the head of the army, 313 emirs were appointed; the emirate consisted of 12 ranks, the highest of which was amir ul-umara. Each chief had a deputy: in the event of a death in battle, a deputy would immediately take his place. The rank was awarded on the basis of courage: a soldier who showed courage in battle for the first time became a десятник, a second time — a сотник, a third time — a тысячник. The victorious emir was awarded an honorary title, a banner, and a drum. In the "Regulations of Temur," the maintenance of each soldier was clearly defined, and the salary was equal to the value of the horse the soldier rode; it was commanded not to deprive elderly sipais of their salary. Religious and racial discrimination was not permitted in the army.

### Tactical news

The textbook emphasizes the innovations introduced by Amir Temur in military art: the deployment of troops on the battlefield in seven corps and the creation of a powerful reserve; the introduction of units that protect the flanks of the troops from enemy attacks, as well as attacking the enemy from the rear; special infantry units fighting in mountainous areas; siege equipment—stone-throwing machines, wall-breaking equipment, and firearms (rad and radandoz, which were first used in 1379 during the siege of Urgench); a detachment of master bridge builders and shipbuilders.

The 19th-century Russian military historian M.I. Ivanin gave such an assessment: "At a time when no nation yet knew how to properly organize its army and waged chaotic battles, Timur had a well-organized regular army with a strong reserve." Before the campaign, a special order of the ruler was announced — tunkal, each unit had its own flag and password (uran). Amir Temur participated in major campaigns with an army of 100-250 thousand people.

## 7. Diplomacy of the State of Amir Temur

According to the textbook, Amir Temur achieved most of his power through his diplomatic potential. Its foreign policy is based on the principles of peace, consideration of parties' interests, respect for the ambassador under any circumstances, and compliance with international rules. In one of his letters, he writes:

- An ambassador has neither death nor captivity, and the ambassador's duty is to convey the order clearly.

After coming to power, Amir Temur ensured the safety of international caravan routes. Thanks to this, from the 70s of the 14th century, the central branch of the Great Silk Road, which passed through Maveraunnahr, was revived. Roads, sardobas, caravanserais, and bridges were repaired, and trading stations—"yams"—were organized every 25-30 km.

### Relations with Egypt and China

Between 1385 and 1405, Amir Temur sent embassies and groups of messengers to Egypt 20 times. The Mamluk Sultan Barkuk often treated the ambassadors rudely; In 1393, he ordered the ambassadors to be killed. Even in such a situation, Amir Temur was patient. According to the treaty concluded in 1401 near Damascus, Sultan Faraj recognized the supreme rule of Amir Temur and released the imprisoned ambassadors. Diplomatic relations with the Ming state in China began in 1387; In 1387-1397, Amir Temur sent ambassadors to China six times, and in 1394, 200 pedigree horses were presented to the emperor. However, the fact that the emperor called Amir Temur "my son" in his message and demanded recognition of China's supreme power caused a cooling of relations.

### Relations with European countries

The strengthening of the Ottoman Empire (1389 Battle of Kosovo, 1396 Battle of Nikopol) created a real threat to Europe. Therefore, the ambassadors of Byzantium, Genoa, Venice, France, and Spain (Castile and Leon) frequently visited the palace of Amir Temur. After the victory of Ankara, Amir Temur sent the Catholic Archbishop John III from Sultaniya to France as his ambassador. In a letter addressed to the King of France, Charles VI, he proposed creating conditions for free trade between the merchants of the two countries and concluding a treaty; the letter ended with the words: "the world will prosper with merchants." Charles VI supported these proposals on June 15, 1403.

## 8. Amir Temur's spirituality

The textbook emphasizes Amir Temur's forgiveness and mercy. For example, he twice forgave the betrayal of the leader Alibek Jankurbani and took him into service again; He also forgave Tokhtamysh Khan twice. In the "Regulations," it is written: "To the good I did good, and to the bad I gave them to evil." And he who was an enemy to me and then came to me repentant and seeking protection, I forgave his enmity and showed him favor and friendship.

Amir Temur listened to the advice of his teachers Shamsiddin Kulol, Mir Said Baraka, and Abu Bakr Tayabadi; he respected scholars and theologians. As Ibn Arabshah writes, "Amir Temur was kind to scholars and showed full respect to the ulema and sages." He was an opponent of fanaticism and sectarianism; he did not allow oppression of non-believers. Regarding the order and discipline in the state, the Sahibkiran says: "I have established order and discipline so that not a single boy will be harmed if he walks with a golden bowl on his head from one end of my kingdom to the other."

## 9. Cultural life during the era of Amir Temur.

The end of Mongol rule, the cessation of internal conflicts, and the formation of a strong, centralized state created a foundation for the rise of cultural life. Creativity has risen to the level of state policy. The inscription on the portal of the Oksaroy Palace in Shahrisabz testifies to this: "If you doubt our strength and power, look at the buildings we have built!"

### Urban planning

The main part of the cities during this period was called "hisar." For example, the wall of the Kesh fortress was built between 1378 and 1379. Samarkand had six gates: Sheikhzada, Ohanin, Feruza, Suzangaron, Korizgoh, and Chorsu; the main streets branched off from the central Registan square. Clavijo writes: "The city of Samarkand is located on a plain... the entire city is surrounded by gardens and vineyards." In 1404, Amir Temur decided to level and widen the city's main streets.

### Architectural monuments

The late 14th and early 15th centuries were a period of flourishing architectural art. Fired brick, stone, and marble were widely used in construction, and the technique of dome formation was improved. Glazed tiles, embedded patterns, ganch and wood carving, and wall painting were used in the decoration; blue and golden colors predominated. Along with masters from Shiraz, Isfahan, and Tabriz, local architects—Fakhri Ali, Usta Zayniddin, and Usta Ali Nasafi—worked on the construction.

The construction of the Aksaray in Shahrisabz began in the spring of 1380, with masters relocated from Khorezm working there. The total height was 70 meters at the time, and the surviving part is 38 meters. Nizamiddin Shami wrote: "His vaults and vaults reach the bear star." The Shahizinda complex in Samarkand was expanded during the reign of Amir Temur. The Gur-Emir Mausoleum is a complex built in 1403 for the sudden death of the grandson of Muhammad Sultan, which later became the tomb of the Timurids: here are buried Amir Temur himself, his sons Miranshah and Shahruh, the grandson of Mirzo Ulugbek, the teacher of Mir Said Baraka. The Bibi Khanum Cathedral Mosque was built in 1399-1404: according to Sharafiddin Ali Yazdi, 500 masons from Azerbaijan, Iran, and India worked here. The Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmad Yasawi (Turkestan) was begun in 1397 by order of Amir Temur and completed in two years.

### Gardens, Art, and Science

Outside the city, residence and recreation gardens were established: Bogi Naqshijahon, Bogi Behisht (1378), Bogi Dilkusho (1396-1399), Bogi Shamol (1397), Bogi Davlatabad (1399), Bogi Chinor, and Bogi Baland. Villages named after Shiraz, Sultaniya, Damascus, Egypt, and Baghdad were built around Samarkand.

During the reign of Amir Temur, a unique school of miniature art was formed in Samarkand, and cobalt carving emerged as an innovation in pottery. Amir Temur invited famous scholars Sa'duddin Taftazani and Sayyid Sharif Jurjani to Samarkand. Sayyid Nasimi, Sayfi Sarai, and Lutfi wrote in Turkic, while Hafiz Shirazi and Kamal Khujandi wrote in Persian; In 1387, Amir Temur met with Hafiz Shirazi in Shiraz.

## Key concepts

- **Amir** — the official title of Amir Temur; Kuragon is the son-in-law of the khan's house.
- A Kurultai is a large council where the most important issues are considered.
- **Dargoh** — supreme state institution; divan - executive power; divanbegi - head of the divan.
- Arzbegi is an official who delivers complaints to the ruler; tavochi is an official who gathers troops.
- **Ulus** — a large territory granted to princes; daruga - the head of the city and district; kutvol - the commandant of the fortress.
- Muhtasib is the overseer of the market and the rules of Sharia.
- Sarbadars—participants of the movement against the Mongol yoke.
- **Tuman** — a ten-thousand-strong army; tunkal - an order to gather an army.
- **Raiyat** — common people; sipah - army; yom - a stop on a caravan route.
