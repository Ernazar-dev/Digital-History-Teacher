# The Ashtarkhanid State in the First Half of the 17th-18th Centuries

> The lecture is based on Topic 4 of the textbook "History of Uzbekistan" by B. Zh. Eshov and A. A. Odilov (Book II, 2020), as well as primary sources from the Ashtarkhanid period.

**Objective of the lecture:** to provide students with a comprehensive understanding of the Ashtarkhanid dynasty's rise to power, the reigns of its key rulers, its system of state governance, economic life, and the development of cities, science, and culture; and to develop skills in analyzing the causes and consequences of political fragmentation.

## 1. Sources and the Ashtarkhanids' rise to power

### Main sources

When studying the history of the Ashtarkhanid period, a number of historical works written in Persian are of great importance. Among them, a special place is occupied by Muhammad Yusuf Munshi's "Tarikh-i Muqim-khani," Mahmud ibn Wali's "Bahr al-Asrar," Khoja Samandar Tirmidhi's "Dastur al-Muluk," Mir Muhammad Amin-i Bukhari's "Ubaydullanama," Abdurrahman Tali's "Tarikh-i Abulfayz-khani," and Muhammad Wafa Karminagi's "Tuhfat al-Khani." As these works were written by court historians, they not only glorify the rulers but also reveal the internal contradictions of the era. The history of the period has been examined in studies by R. G. Mukminova, B. A. Akhmedov, Kh. Z. Ziyoev, G. A. Agzamova, and other scholars.

### The End of the Shaybanid Dynasty

Following the death of Abdullah Khan II in 1598, his son Abdul-Mu'min ascended the throne, but he was killed at the end of that same year. The next ruler, Pir Muhammad II (1598-1601), was likewise unable to govern the country and was killed in 1601 in battle against Baqi Muhammad. In the south, Shah Abbas I of Iran captured Sabzevar, Mashhad, and Herat. As Muhammad Yusuf Munshi wrote, "lawlessness reigned in Mawara'unnahr, and no one was willing to obey anyone." This situation paved the way for a new dynasty to come to power.

### Who Were the Ashtarkhanids?

The Ashtarkhanids were descendants of the Genghisids and, beginning in the 1480s, ruled the Astrakhan (Ashtarkhan) Khanate in the Volga region. In the mid-16th century, after Ivan the Terrible conquered the khanate, Yar Muhammad Sultan, a representative of the dynasty, arrived in Bukhara with his family. The ruler of Bukhara, Iskandar Sultan, gave them a warm reception; Yar Muhammad's son, Janibek, married Zuhrabegim, the daughter of Sultan Iskandar. They had three sons: Din Muhammad, Baqi Muhammad, and Vali Muhammad.

In 1601, seeking to put an end to lawlessness, the Bukhara nobility invited Janibek Sultan to take the throne. He abdicated in favor of his eldest son, Din Muhammad, who subsequently died in a clash with the Iranian army. Following this, the ruler of Samarkand, Baqi Muhammad (1601-1605), ascended the Bukharan throne. Historical sources thus mark this as the beginning of the Ashtarkhanid (rulers from Hajji Tarkhan) or Janid (named after Janibek Sultan) dynasty, which lasted for a century and a half.

### Baqi Muhammad Khan and Vali Muhammad Khan

Although coins were initially minted in the name of Janibek Sultan, actual power was held by Baqi Muhammad. He sought to put an end to internal fragmentation: in 1602, he captured Balkh and granted it to his brother Vali Muhammad, and he subjugated Andkhoy, Shibarghan, Maymana, Baghlan, Badakhshan, and Hissar. Munshi wrote that he "reviewed the posts of officials, oversaw the order of the army, and the condition of the subjects"; however, because of his brief reign, the reforms bore no fruit.

In 1605, the ruler of Balkh, Vali Muhammad Khan (1605-1611), ascended the throne. In 1607, he concluded a peace treaty with Abbas I, and Khorasan passed entirely into the hands of the Safavids. In 1608, disgruntled officials hatched a conspiracy, and the khan fled to Iran. In 1611, with the support of Abbas I, he reclaimed the throne, but in October of that same year, he died in a clash with Imam Quli Khan near Samarkand.

## 2. The period of Imam Quli Khan — relative stability

The most powerful ruler of the dynasty was the son of Baqi Muhammad Khan, Imam Quli Khan (1611-1642). By that time, the high-handedness of local rulers had reached its peak; Imam Quli Khan managed to weaken their movement.

### Domestic policy

Although Imam Quli Khan was unable to recover Khwarazm and Khorasan, he continued the fight against the Kazakhs, Kalmyks, and other nomads who threatened Tashkent, Andijan, and Turkestan; occasionally allying with them, he directed his forces against Iran. In Tashkent, he appointed his son, Iskandar Sultan, as ruler; outraged by his plundering, the residents of Tashkent revolted and killed him. Historical sources note with regret that, in retaliation, Imam Quli Khan carried out a massacre in Tashkent in 1613. In alliance with the Dzungars (Kalmyks), he dealt a crushing blow to the Kazakh Khanate in the Talas Valley. However, the Dzungar (Oirat) Khanate, founded in 1635, subsequently joined forces with the Kazakhs to launch frequent raids against the Khanate of Bukhara.

### Bare-bellied

A loyal official of Imamquli Khan, Yalangtush Biy Ataliq (of the Alchin clan), served as the ruler of Samarkand for many years. Possessing immense wealth and his own army, he governed Samarkand almost autonomously. According to the author of "Bahr al-Asrar," the treasury of the ruler of Samarkand rivaled that of the khan himself. It was by his order that the renowned madrasas of Sher-Dor (1619-1636) and Tillya-Kori (1641-1646) were constructed in Samarkand.

### Prices and Foreign Policy

In "Tarikh-i Muqim-khani," Munshi compares Imamquli Khan to Sultan Husayn Bayqara, writing that during his reign "there was neither unrest nor rebellion in Balkh or Bukhara." According to the source, the khan "eased the burdens of the poor, did not turn away those who came with petitions," and walked the bazaars and mahallas at night dressed in simple clothes to learn firsthand about the condition of the common people. The historian Hafiz Tanish Bukhari and Abu al-Ghazi Bahadur Khan, who later became the Khan of Khiva, both served at his court. In 1621, a new copy of Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi's "Zafarnama" was transcribed.

External ties reflect the khanate's relations with neighboring states. Relations with India strengthened during the reign of the Mughal emperor Jahangir (1605-1627): in 1625, an ambassador from the Juybar sheikhs, Haji Abdul-Rahim, brought gifts to India, including a ruby valued at the tribute of an entire province, and presented a plan for the partition of Khorasan. As for Russia, in 1619 the khan's envoy Odambiy was received by Mikhail Romanov in Moscow; in response, Ivan Khokhlov's embassy visited the khanate in 1620-1622 and gathered extensive information. Despite border clashes with Iran, trade relations continued to develop.

In 1642, Imam Quli Khan, having gone blind toward the end of his life, abdicated the throne and set out on the Hajj; he was given a grand reception in Iran. He died in 1644 in Medina.

## 3. Intensification of Political Fragmentation (1642-1711)

### Nadir Muhammad Khan (1642-1645)

Nadir Muhammad, the younger brother of Imam Quli Khan, who had ruled Balkh for many years, could not find common ground with the nobility. He curtailed the power of the emirs and divided the provinces among his six sons, a grandson, and two nephews (Samarkand to Abdulaziz, Balkh to Subhan Quli, Tashkent to Bahram, etc.). In April 1645, discontented officials proclaimed his eldest son, Abdulaziz Sultan, as khan; Nadir Muhammad fled to Balkh.

### Abdulaziz Khan (1645-1681)

Nadir Muhammad turned to the Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan for help to regain power; the Mughals captured Balkh and Badakhshan and ruled them for two years. In October 1647, Abdulaziz Khan recaptured Balkh. However, the conflict in Balkh with his younger brother, Subhankuli Sultan (1652-1657), intensified, and Balkh was effectively governed independently. The Khivan khans raided frequently: in 1658, they sacked Karmana and Vardonzi, and in 1662, they reached Bukhara. According to sources, "Mawarannahr and Balkh were on the verge of ruin due to the attacks of the Khwarazmians and Kazakhs"; the autonomy of large landowners weakened the central government. In 1681, Abdulaziz Khan ceded the throne to Subhankuli Khan and set out on the Hajj, during which he died.

### Subhankuli Khan (1681-1702)

Subhankuli Khan's reign was a tumultuous and contradictory period. The Khivan khan, Anusha Khan, launched predatory raids into the Zarafshan and Kashkadarya oases; irrigation networks were disrupted, and severe inflation set in. To replenish the treasury, the khan introduced additional levies and collected taxes several years in advance. With the help of the hakim of Badakhshan, Mahmud-biy Atalyk (of the Qataghan clan), the Khivans were expelled from Samarkand. In 1686, as a result of a conspiracy, Anusha Khan was blinded with a hot needle. In 1688, in reward for his services, Mahmud-biy was granted Balkh-a territory traditionally reserved for heirs to the throne; later, Mahmud-biy ceased obeying the khan.

Subhankuli Khan temporarily subjugated the Khiva Khanate; he exchanged letters with the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, an Indian ambassador arrived in 1689, an envoy was sent to Russia in 1690, and an Ottoman ambassador, Mustafa Chavush, was received in 1691. He patronized public works and urban improvement, and wrote poetry under the pen name "Nishoniy."

### Ubaydullah Khan (1702-1711)

Ubaydullah Khan was the last Ashtarkhanid ruler to fight against political fragmentation. After a long siege, he captured Balkh and ordered his troops' horses not to trample the peasants' crops. He dismissed many officials, appointing sons of artisans and merchants in their place (such as the low-born Turaquli Kushbegi), and abolished the tax exemptions of the Juybari sheikhs.

In 1708, he carried out a monetary reform: every silver coin was melted down to mint four new coins, which were decreed to be of equal value to the old ones. The merchants closed their shops after refusing to accept the new coins, and an uprising broke out in Bukhara. These actions intensified public discontent. Influenced by Mahmud Biy, the ruler of Balkh, Muhammad Muqim Sultan, began to lay claim to supreme power. Ubaydullah Khan built a madrasa and the Charvak Khanabad garden estate in Bukhara. He was assassinated in 1711 as a result of a conspiracy. This event showed that reforms aimed at centralizing the state were doomed to failure if implemented without strong social support.

## 4. The Era of Abulfayz Khan and the Crisis of the Dynasty

The sixteen-year-old Abulfayz Khan (1711-1747) was elevated to the throne. The governors of Bukhara, Samarkand, Tashkent, Balkh, Shahrisabz, and Badakhshan began to act independently; the emirs exploited the khan for their own ends, turning him into a puppet ruler. In 1716-1717, a hundred-thousand-strong host of Khitai-Kipchak tribes settled in the vicinity of Miyankal and Karshi, and widespread looting ensued. In the 1720s, Kazakh sultans fleeing Dzungar persecution invaded the Zarafshan oasis and subsequently settled in the region as well.

Muhammad Hakim-biy, an ataliq of the Manghit clan, sought power. To oppose him, Ibrahim-biy Kenagas, the ruler of Shahrisabz, proclaimed Rajab Sultan the ruler of Samarkand in 1722 and declared himself "emir of emirs." Having failed to capture Bukhara, Rajab Sultan turned to the Kazakhs for aid; between 1723 and 1729, the Kazakhs continually raided the Zarafshan and Kashkadarya oases. Neglecting state affairs, Abulfayz Khan indulged in pleasures and handed over governance to the Kalmyk kushbegi Javshan. Samarkand, Badakhshan, Karshi, Balkh, and Fergana were effectively controlled by autonomous rulers.

### Nadir Shah's Campaign

Nadir Shah (1736-1747), who founded the Afsharid state in Iran, took advantage of this fragmentation. In 1736, his son Riza Quli besieged Qarshi, but retreated when the Khan of Khiva, Ilbars Khan, came to the city's aid. In 1740, Nadir Shah himself crossed the Amu Darya with a large army. Muhammad Hakim Biy entered his service, and Abulfayz Khan surrendered and signed a treaty of vassalage: Bukhara was to supply Iran with wheat and barley for eight years and send an army of 10,000 men (commanded by Hakim Biy's son, Muhammad Rahim Biy). Hakim Biy became the kushbegi (chief vizier) and effectively seized power.

### The End of the Dynasty

Following Hakim Biy's death in 1743, all power passed to his son Muhammad Rahim Biy. In 1747, Abulfayz Khan was assassinated in a conspiracy; under the subsequent Ashtarkhanids-Abdulmumin, Ubaydullah Khan II, and Shergazi Khan-actual power likewise remained with Rahim Biy. In December 1756, Muhammad Rahim Biy officially took the throne, marking the beginning of the Manghit dynasty in Bukhara.

## 5. State System and Administrative Governance

The state system of the Ashtarkhanids differed little from that of the Shaybanid period: power, both at the center and in the provinces, was largely concentrated in a single institution-the dargah-while semi-nomadic customs persisted alongside sedentary traditions.

### The Khan and Officials

Officially, the khan was the supreme ruler: decrees were issued in his name, coins were minted with his imprint, and his name was invoked in the khutbah. In practice, however, many khans were mere puppets in the hands of influential dignitaries and the ulama. The state apparatus included such offices as the naqib, atalyk, parwanachi, dadkhah, diwanbegi, qushbegi, chukhrabashi, yasaul, inaq, qurchi, eshikaghabashi, mirzabashi, and dastarkhanchi.

While in the 16th century the atalyk was primarily tied to local administration, from the reign of Abdulaziz Khan onward, his influence at the center grew significantly. In the "Ubaydulla-name," the atalyk is described as "umdat al-umara"-the "pillar of the entire emir class." It was the atalyks Hakim-biy and Rahim-biy who ultimately seized power. The status of the qushbegi rose under Ubaydullah Khan and Abulfayz Khan: Turaquli qushbegi held the authority to make official appointments and issue written directives without royal charters; in the source, the qushbegi is referred to as "the greatest of the emirs."

### Center and Provinces

Administration was two-tiered: central and local. After Bukhara, the most influential city was Balkh, which was usually ruled by the heir to the throne. The subordination of regional rulers to the central government was limited to remitting an annual tax to the treasury and participating in military campaigns at the khan's command; most rulers maintained their own administrative systems and armies. Early 18th-century sources mention six provinces subordinate to the khanate: Bukhara, Samarkand, Sagaradj, Ura-Tyube, Shahrisabz, and Khuzar. Hissar, Shahrisabz, and Fergana were virtually semi-independent; during the reign of Abulfayz Khan, Khiva and Kokand were separate states.

### Religious Offices

In the administration, the sheikh-ul-islam, sadr, qazi-kalan, and khoja played prominent roles. The main religious offices were filled by candidates from the Juybari Khojas. The sheikh-ul-islam served as a permanent adviser to the ruler on all matters. Each vilayet qazi adjudicated religious, family, inheritance, and criminal cases, while a dedicated qazi-askar dealt with the military. In legal matters, a special place belonged to the mufti, who was well versed in fiqh and hadith. In the late 17th and early 18th centuries, the influence of dervish orders grew.

## 6. Agriculture and Landownership

### Farming

Political fragmentation negatively affected agriculture; 18th-century sources report the flight of peasants from the Zeravshan and Fergana regions, who abandoned their lands. At the beginning of the 17th century, many irrigated lands were given to the Joybari sheikhs; although water could not be sold under Sharia law, wealthy landowners controlled the canals. Under Imam Quli Khan, canals were dug in Qarshi (1614-1615) and Koshkurgan (1633-1634), but the scale of these works was limited. In the late 1680s, due to Khivan raids, the water supply was disrupted, and famine began in Samarkand, Qarshi, and Fergana; according to the source, "the dehkans did not even have millet or wheat bread."

The main agricultural region was the Zeravshan oasis. Wheat, barley, sorghum, beans, mung beans, rice, cotton, vegetables, and melons were cultivated. Melon and gourd crops, especially sweet melons, were highly developed; the "Baburnama" states that "nowhere in Maveranannahr are there as many good melons as in Bukhara." A large portion of the irrigated land was occupied by orchards and vineyards.

### Animal husbandry

Animal husbandry became the leading sector of the economy. According to Babur, wealthy aristocrats owned up to 30,000-40,000 sheep. Karakul pelts were produced in Karakul, and black and blue pelts in Qarshi. Sheep breeding was the primary source of wealth for the semi-nomadic population; Karabair horses, Bactrian camels, wool, and hides were in high demand on both domestic and foreign markets.

### Forms of land tenure

Although land distribution was officially in the hands of the khan, in practice, much of the land was controlled by high-ranking officials and the clergy.

| Form | Content |
|---|---|
| Sultan's possessions | State lands; peasants using them paid kharaj |
| Suyurgal | A gift to princes, ulema, and sayyids; no tax was paid to the treasury |
| Solitary | For the military and officials-not the land, but the right to collect taxes from it |
| Property of a free person impartially | Property granted for special services, exempt from taxation |
| Property | Private land; often leased to landless peasants |
| Waqf | Tax-exempt lands granted to religious institutions |

In the 17th-18th centuries, the nature of the suyurgal changed: it was granted primarily to Muslim clergy, sayyids, sheikhs, ulema, and poets. Mahmud ibn Wali writes: "If the ruler grants land to the ulema and sayyids, it is called a suyurgal; if it is granted to the military, it is called a tanha."

## 7. Cities, crafts, and trade

Trade, crafts, science, and culture were concentrated mainly in the cities. The most important centers were Samarkand, Bukhara, and Tashkent. Samarkand was famous for its specialized markets; according to the source, "there was a separate market for each commodity, and they did not mix with one another." There were markets of potters (kuzagars), blacksmiths, jewelers, skullcap makers, butchers, and dyers. The famous Samarkand paper workshops were situated on the banks of the Siyab Canal.

Tashkent was renowned for its trade in mineral resources and with the Kazakh steppes; outside the city lay the rulers' residence, Charvak Kayvus, and caravanserais were also built along the mountain road by the Chirchiq River. The city walls repeatedly protected the population from nomad raids. Bukhara was the capital, encircled by a high wall and divided into three parts: "two parts belonged to the ruler, and one part to the merchants and the market." The city was home to numerous caravanserais, tims (covered markets), and bathhouses.

Due to political fragmentation and the existence of semi-independent domains, trade links between cities were weak. The subsistence economy expanded, and essential handicraft goods began to be produced even in large villages (*kishlaks*). The main craft sectors were textiles, pottery, and woodworking. In textiles, Samarkand, Bukhara, Karshi, and Shahrisabz were renowned for their *alacha* (striped fabric), coarse calico, chintz, and velvet, with cotton and silk serving as the primary raw materials. Potters were divided into dish-makers, jug-makers, and producers of construction materials (bricks and *tandoors*). Woodworkers made doors, windows, carts, chests, cradles, and boats, while the finest craftsmen engaged in wood carving. In Bukhara alone, artisans practiced about 200 distinct crafts.

## 8. Science, Literature, and Architecture

### Historiography and Education

Works such as "Bahr al-Asrar," "Tarikh-i Muqimkhani," "Dastur al-Muluk," "Ubaydullanama," "Tuhfat al-Khani," and "Tarikh-i Abulfayz-Khan" were written during this period. Alongside praising the khan, these works correctly recognized that the crisis was the result of political fragmentation and internecine warfare, advancing the idea that the way out lay in the centralization of power and just governance.

Children began attending school between the ages of 5 and 7, and their education lasted for 5 to 8 years. Boys studied in private schools affiliated with mosques and madrasas, while girls were taught at home by an otin-oyi (female tutor). Expenses were covered by revenue from waqf endowments and parental contributions, while orphaned children studied free of charge. The curriculum included the Haftiyak, arithmetic, the works of Navoi, Fuzuli, Bedil, Mashrab, and Sufi Allayar, as well as the Chor Kitob. Studies at a madrasa lasted 7 to 12 years; there were more than 150 madrasas in the khanate. Subjects taught there included fiqh (Islamic jurisprudence), astronomy, geometry, medicine, history, geography, literature, and calligraphy.

### Thinkers and poets

The philosopher Yusuf Karabaghi (1563-1647), who worked in Samarkand, advanced the idea that "the Universe is a single being, and everything in existence is part of it." Muhammad Sharif al-Bukhari authored about twenty works and identified four types of soul: natural, vegetative, animal, and human. Mirzo Abdulkadir Bedil (1644-1721), a native of Shakhrisabz descended from the Barlas clan, worked in India, composed more than 120,000 verses, and was nicknamed "Abdulmaani" ("Father of Meanings"). The artisan weaver and poet Saido Nasafi (1637-1710) wrote about representatives of 212 different professions, praised craftsmen, and authored the work "Bahoriyot" ("Springtime"). Sufi Allayar's (1644-1724) work "Maslak al-Muttaqin" (comprising 12,000 bayts) was used as a textbook in madrasas. Turdi Faroghi, a poet from the Yuz tribe, condemned the oppression of officials in his poems and called for unity.

Four major tazkiras were produced in Bukhara (by Mutribi, Maliho Samarkandi, Mulla Sadyk Samarkandi, and Nurmuhammad Nasafi); Mutribi's tazkira alone contains information on more than 320 poets. Notably, many artists were craftsmen: Gafur Samarkandi was a haberdasher, Masiho was a blacksmith, and Fitrat Samarkandi was a gold embroiderer. In mathematics, treatises were written on inheritance distribution.

### Architecture

Distinctive features of regional architectural schools are evident in Khwarazm, Transoxiana, Fergana, and Tashkent. In Samarkand, the Sher-Dor Madrasah (facing the Ulugh Beg Madrasah and named for the depiction of two lions on its portal) was built by Yalangtush Bakhodur, along with the Tilya-Kori Madrasah ("gilded," which also served as a congregational Friday mosque). Together with the Ulugh Beg Madrasah, they formed the Registan ensemble. In Bukhara, the Abdulaziz Khan Madrasah (featuring marble slabs and glazed tilework), the Darush-shifa hospital built by Subhan Quli Khan, and the Lyabi-Khauz complex built by Nadir Divan-begi were erected. The madrasahs of Nadir Muhammad Khan and Subhan Quli Khan were built in Balkh, the Arab Muhammad Khan Madrasah in Khiva, the Khoja Ilm complex near Shahrisabz, and others. These structures testify that, despite the fraught political situation, ancient traditions persisted in the architecture of Transoxiana.

## Conclusion

The Ashtarkhanid period (1601-1756) was characterized by arbitrariness, internecine strife, and local rulers defying central authority. Although Imam Quli Khan, Subhan Quli Khan, and Ubaydullah Khan sought to strengthen central rule, the influence of officials like the atalyk and kushbegi, along with large landowners and the Juybari Khojas, continued to grow. The secession of Khiva and Kokand, nomadic raids, and the invasion by Nadir Shah weakened the state, leading to the transfer of power to the Manghits. Foreign relations were confined to neighboring territories, and the country remained isolated from global technological progress. Despite this, significant achievements were made in architecture, historiography, literature, and Islamic jurisprudence.

## Key Concepts

- **Ashtarkhanids (Janids)** — a Genghisid dynasty originating from Astrakhan, 1601-1756.
- **Atalyk** — "pillar of the emirate/nobility," an empowered senior office.
- **Kushbegi** — an office equivalent to prime minister.
- **Dargah** — the central administration/court.
- **Suyurghal, Tankhwah, Mulk-i Sultani, Waqf, Mulk-i Hurr-i Khalis** — forms of land tenure.
- **Juybari Khojas** — an influential religious and political dynasty/house.
- **Tazkira** — a biographical anthology of poets.
