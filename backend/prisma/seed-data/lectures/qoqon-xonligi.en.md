# Kokand Khanate. Political, socio-economic, and cultural life

## 1. Sources and Formation of the Khanate

### Sources

The history of the Khanate of Kokand is covered in works such as Muhammad Hakimkhan Tura's "Muntahab ut-tavorix," Mulla Niyaz Muhammad's "Tarihi Shahruhi," Mirza Olim Mushrif's "Ansob us-salotin va tavorih ul-havokin," Muhammad Salih Tashkendi's "Tarihi jadidai Tashkent," Ishaqhan Ibrat's "Tarihi Farg'ona," and Avaz Muhammad Attar's "Tarihi Jahonnomai." This era has been studied by R.N. Nabiev, Kh.Z. Ziyoev, Sh.Kh. Vakhidov, G.A. Agzamova, and others, as well as by Kyrgyz, Turkish, Korean, and English scholars. Kokand historiography is distinguished by its poetic style and proximity to the palace environment.

### Formation of the khanate

At the beginning of the 18th century, the disorder in the Ashtarkhanid state, the inability to defend territories, and the attacks of the Dzungars led to the formation of a separate state separate from Bukhara in the Fergana Valley. The reason for this was the uprising of the Chodak Khojas against the Ashtarkhanids in 1704. The Ming leader Shah Rukhbi (1709-1721) forcibly seized power from the Chodak Khojas and established the Ming dynasty in the Fergana Valley in 1709 or 1710. The residence was Tepakorgan. In his hands were the lands of Kokand, Namangan, Margilan, Konibodom, Isfara, and their surroundings. Initially, Shah Rukhbi received the title of atalyk from the Khan of Bukhara, meaning he was not entirely independent.

### Golden Cradle Legend

Shahrukhbi's origin is linked to the legend of the "Golden Cradle": it is said that in 1512, Babur gave birth to a son in Fergana and left him in the valley; the child grew up in the Ming clan and received the name "Golden Cradle." Shah Rukhbi called himself his descendant. The textbook emphasizes that this is a fabricated legend, and there is no hint of it in "Baburnama." It served to justify claims to power and demonstrate superiority over other clans. This example shows how rulers used historical legends to legitimize their power.

## 2. XVIII century rulers

Abdurahimbi (1721–1733) annexed Andijan in 1724, Khujand in 1725, and Ura-Tyube in 1726; In 1732, he briefly captured Samarkand and Kattaqurgan, threatening Shahrisabz. In 1733, he was killed in an assassination attempt. Abdulkarimbi (1733-1750) moved the capital from Tepakorgan to Kokand, surrounded the city with a wall, and built the gates of Isfara, Katagan, Margilan, and Haydarbek. Having repelled the attacks of the Dzungars (Kalmyks) in 1741–1745, the khanate retained its independence. The heroism of a young man named Batyr Qipchaq in the defense of Kokand inspired the entire army.

Erdanabi (1751-1752, 1753-1764) is described as a brave and just ruler; In 1758, he took Ura-Tyube. In 1762, when Chinese ambassadors demanded help for the army, he opposed this and turned to the ruler of Afghanistan, Ahmad Shah Durrani, for help. Narbutabi (1764–1800) strengthened central power, suppressed uprisings in Chust and Namangan, and subjugated Andijan, Osh, and Khujand (his campaign against Tashkent in 1799 ended in failure). Under him, a small coin—the fulus—was introduced into circulation, and new irrigation ditches were dug; China recognized him as an independent ruler.

## 3. Alimkhan, Umarkhan, and Muhammad Alikhan

### Alimkhan (1800-1810)

If the previous rulers ruled with the title of "biy," then Alimkhan officially received the title of "khan" and in 1805 introduced the name of the state — Kokand Khanate. He created a strong mercenary army, subjugated the Ohangaron oasis, Tashkent, Chimkent, and Turkestan, and made campaigns against Jizzakh and Zomin. For trade with Russia, he built the fortress of Akmechet on the banks of the Syr Darya. The murder of his brother Rustambek and several commanders and religious figures led to a conspiracy: he was killed in the Altykush area on the way from Tashkent in 1810. His brother Umarbek led the conspiracy.

### Umar Khan (1810-1822)

Umar Khan established strict order in state administration; He introduced titles and positions in imitation of the era of Amir Temur and Husayn Bayqara. In 1818, he completed the construction of the Great Mosque of Kokand and, with the consent of the ulema, received the title of "amir al-muslimin"; in the same year, he introduced the position of mingbashi and the position of sheikh-ul-islam. He sent ambassadors to Khiva, China, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia; In 1813, the Russian ambassador F. Nazarov arrived. Turkestan (1815) and Ura-Tyube (1817); On the banks of the Syr Darya, he built the fortifications of Yangi Kurgan, Julek, Kamyshkurgan, and Koshkurgan. Historians described Umar Khan as a just and pious ruler. He wrote poetry under the pseudonym "Amiri," gathered more than 70 poets in the palace; his wife is the famous poetess Nodirabegim.

### Muhammad Ali Khan (Madalikhan, 1822-1842)

Under him, the territory of the khanate became the largest: part of the Kyrgyz lands was annexed, and his authority was recognized by Kölb, Hisar, Badakhshan, Darvaz, and Mastcha. In 1826–1831, he carried out campaigns against Kashgar and resettled 70,000 Uyghurs to Tashkent and the Fergana Valley; the ulema gave him the title "Gazi." Subsequently, he began to persecute officials, executed his experienced chief advisor, Mingbashi Haqquli, in 1840, and remained indifferent to state affairs. Dissatisfied officials turned to the Emir of Bukhara, Nasrullah, for help.

## 4. The crisis of the mid-19th century

### Conquest of Bukhara (1842)

In April 1842, Amir Nasrullah captured Kokand; Muhammad Ali Khan was executed along with part of his family, and the poetess Nadirabegim was also killed in this massacre. The oppression of the ruler of Bukhara, Ibrahim Dadha, who remained in Kokand, caused an uprising: the Kipchaks led by Sherali Khan (1842-1845), a relative of Muhammad Ali Khan, expelled the Bukharians. Nasrullah's new campaign in the autumn of 1842 was repelled by the enterprise of Muslimkuli the Kipchak.

### Khudoyarkhan and the Kipchaks

In 1845, Musulmankuli installed Sherali Khan's 16-year-old son, Khudoyarkhan (1845-1853, 1863, 1865-1875), on the throne and effectively ruled the country himself. The textbook identifies four main causes of the crisis: the fact that the sedentary population did not recognize the power of the nomadic and semi-nomadic Kipchaks; the struggle of the Kipchaks among themselves; the disdainful attitude of the Kipchaks toward the sedentary population and other peoples; and the external factor—the gradual invasion of the Russian Empire into the territory of the khanate.

In 1853, relying on the sedentary landowners and the Tashkent nobility, Khudoyar Khan executed Musulmanquli, but the struggle for the throne did not cease: in 1858, he was overthrown by Mallahan (killed in 1863), then Shalimurad ascended the throne, and from 1863, Sultan Said Khan (1863-1865); Khudayar Khan fled to Bukhara twice. Taking advantage of internal contradictions, Russia captured Turkestan and Chimkent in 1864, and on June 17, 1865, after a long siege, it captured Tashkent. Eventually, the khanate was abolished, and in 1876, the Fergana region was established in its place.

## 5. Administrative structure and population

At the end of the 18th century, the khanate consisted only of the Fergana Valley; Under Alim Khan, the possessions of Tashkent (Ahangaran, Chinaz, Turkestan) were added, and under Umar Khan, those of Ura-Tyube. Under Muhammad Ali Khan, the khanate bordered Russia's Siberian region to the north, Bukhara and Khiva to the west, Karategin, Darvaz, and Kölb to the south, and Kashgar to the east. The khanate was divided into bekdoms (sometimes regions, sarkarias); sources provide the names of 15 bekdoms (Kokand, Margilan, Shahrihan, Andijan, Namangan, Sokh, Balykchi, Chust, Koson, etc.). The beks were subordinate vassals of the khan and were required to participate in military campaigns with troops and send gifts. The borders were guarded by the fortresses of Akmechet, Avliyoota, Pishpak, Tokmok, and Niyazbek.

### Population

| Period | Population |
|---|---|
| Early 19th century | 1 million |
| Mid-19th century | 1.5-2 million |
| Second half of the 19th century | 3 million |

If at the beginning of the 19th century more than 40% of the population were nomads and semi-nomads, by the end of the century they had decreased to 15%. The majority were Uzbeks; Tajiks, Kyrgyz, Kipchaks, Karakalpaks, Uyghurs also lived in the vicinity of Andijan, Kazakhs in the vicinity of Tashkent, and Indians, Afghans, and Arabs in the cities.

## 6. State Administration, Positions, and Ranks

State administration was based on the system formed under Amir Temur and reformed under the Shaybanids; the khan's power was unlimited, and he was the largest owner. However, in certain periods, court officials were stronger than the khan—for example, Muslimkuli (Mingbashi and Atalik, 1844–1852) or Alikuli (Amirlashkar, 1863–1865). In the palace, a council headed by a mingbashi considered important matters. The khan's descendants were called khanzades and toras.

### Military rank

The highest post after the khan was Amir ul-Umara. Amirlashkar and mingboshi — the ruler of a domain, who gives a thousand horsemen, a military commander in war, has become the highest position since the time of Sherali Khan. Botirboshi commanded more than 500 warriors and sometimes supervised the construction of irrigation structures. There were also the positions of kushbegi, vali (the khan's deputy in the region), and kalaban (kutval). The kurbashi was the head of the weapons, rifle, and artillery barracks. In the army, there were positions such as topchiboshi, ponsadbashi (500), yuzbashi, panjakhbashi (50), and dahbashi (10).

### Royal and Religious Positions

The palace positions included the atalyk, beklarbegi, divanbegi, treasurer, inak, eshikohasi, parvanachi, dodkhoh (one who delivers citizens' complaints to the khan), dastarkhanchi, shigaul (one who introduces ambassadors), kushbegi, kitabdor, risolachi, and jarchi. They received an annual salary in the form of money, horses, sheep, grain, and land. Religious positions consisted of the positions of sheikh-ul-islam (since 1818), khoja kalan, alam, sudur (calculation of waqf), orak (tax on waqf lands; there was a sign in the form of a sickle on the turban), mudarris, imam, qazi ul-kuzzat (qazi of qazies), qazi kalan, qazi askar, and tarakachi (heritage distributor).

## 7. Land ownership and taxes

### Irrigation and Agriculture

Agriculture formed the basis of the economy. With the increasing demand for yarn and fabrics in the Russian market in the 19th century, large canals were built in the valley: Shahrihansay, Namangan New Aryk, Ulugnakhr, Andijansay, Altyaryk, and others. Small structures were built by the villagers under the leadership of the mirobbashi, while large canals were built under the leadership of the khan or his representative. Grain farming, horticulture, and sericulture were developed; the main food of the poor was corn; In the 19th century, attention to cotton growing intensified. Khujand, Konibodom, Isfara, Sokh, and Rishtan specialize in growing apricots.

### Land ownership

The main forms of land ownership were: kharaj lands — private lands of landowners; amlak (state) lands — "zamini xos" belonging to the khan; private lands (suyurgal) — granted to officials by decree of the khan; waqf lands — belonging to mosques, madrasas and cemeteries. There were also forms of ownership, lease, act (confiscation of property), and tanho (receiving tax revenues from the land as a gift). The tax-free lands of sayids, khojas, sheikhs, and ishans were called "mulki hur."

### Taxes

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Basic according to Sharia | Kharaj is 1/5 of the harvest (5 sacks of grain in 1 sack); ushr is 1/10; zakat is 1/40 of the income or livestock |
| Official | Tanabona (from each tanab of garden, vegetable garden land), khansalik, caravan duties, tax for crossing the Syr Darya, salt tax |
| Traditional | Wedding, inheritance, scales, river crossing, nomadic livestock |
| Extraordinary | Gold money, transportation money, hay money, inclinations, and others |

In addition to taxes, the population was forced to engage in canal digging, ditch cleaning, and construction. The increase in taxes and obligations from year to year intensified the people's dissatisfaction and created a basis for the political crisis of the state.

## 8. Crafts and trade

### Craft

Every city was famous for its products.

| City | Popular craft |
|---|---|
| Kokand | jewelry, paper production, weapons |
| Shahrixon, Chust | sewing, metalworking, knife making |
| Margilan, Namangan, Andijan | silk fabrics |
| Tashkent | sewing, weaving, cast iron, iron and copper production |

Cannons, rifles, swords, and shields were manufactured in Kokand. Craftsmen were divided into the upper class (masters who owned their own workshops and raw materials) and the lower class (day laborers). Gold was mined in the upper reaches of the Syr Darya, on the banks of the Kosonsoy and Chirchiq rivers, iron in the foothills of the Alay, but most of the metals were brought from Russia.

### Trade

Domestic trade was primarily retail. Kokand was one of the commercial centers of all Central Asia, and its markets were distinguished by their abundance of products and cheapness: in the 20s of the 19th century, there were six markets here, and those who betrayed the buyer's rights were severely punished. Foreign trade was conducted with Bukhara, Khiva, Afghanistan, Iran, the Ottomans, India, China (through Koshgar), and especially Russia. Silk and silk fabrics, cotton, leather, precious stones, and jewelry were exported, while tea, metal, porcelain, fabrics, clothing, gold, and silver were brought in. From India, 10-15 thousand camels with cargo arrived annually through Kabul. The growth of Russian industry after 1861 led to the active seizure of the Kokand market by Russia: cotton was mainly exported from the khanate to Russia.

## 9. Cultural life

### Architecture and Applied Arts

In 1842, there were 15 madrasas in Kokand (Mohlar oyim, Muhammad Alikhan, Narbutabiy, Jomi, Sultan Muradbek, and others); four large madrasas were built in the center of Chorsu. More than 200 madrasas were built in the cities of the khanate. The most famous monument is the Palace of Khudoyar Khan (the 19th century). In Tashkent, Chorsu Market unites eight streets.

The Kokand and Tashkent schools of architecture are distinguished by wide landscape plans, elegant decor, bright colors (most often red and green), and woodcarving and ganch carving. Applied arts examples: the Dusti Khudo Mosque in Altyaryk, the Otakouzi Madrasah in Andijan, the Chodak Mosque, and the Khoja Ilgor Mosque in Rishtan. The khanate had its own school of calligraphy (naskh, shikaste, nasta'liq inscriptions).

### Science and historiography

Religious sciences predominated in education, while natural sciences developed primarily in applied fields (medicine, geometry, hydrology). Muhammad Salih Tashkendi highlighted the geography and flora of the oasis in "Tarikh-i Jadidai Tashkent"; Muhammad Hakimkhan Tura described the 1823 Fergana earthquake in detail. The works of the Kokand school of historiography are distinguished by their poetic style: "Tarikh-i Shahruhi," "Umarnama," "Muntahab ut-tawarikh," and "Tarikh-i Jahannama."

### The Literary Environment of Kokand

Representatives of the literary environment created in two directions: court poets and singers of folk sorrow; the image of the Motherland united both. At the beginning of the 19th century, there were 120 schools and 40 madrasas in Kokand, 80 in Margilan, 20 in Tashkent, and about 300 mosques. In 1821, Fazli Namangani, by order of the khan, compiled a collection of poems by 63 poets titled "Majmuai Shoiron."

Nodirabegim (Mohlaroyim, 1792-1842) was born in Andijan; After Umar Khan's death, he took an active part in state affairs, patronizing scholars and calligraphers; Under the pseudonyms Nodira, Komila, and Maknuna, she left behind about 10,000 lines in Uzbek and Tajik. Uvaysi (Jahon Atin, 1780-1841) collaborated at the court with Nadirabegim, writing 269 ghazals, 29 mukhammases, and 3 dastans. Gulkhani, who worked as a stoker in a bathhouse, transformed the fable into an independent genre in Uzbek literature with his work "Zarbulmasal." Makhmur raised social satire to a high level (the poem "Hapalak"). Babarahim Mashrab (1640-1711) was born in Namangan; he condemned the oppression of the ruling elite and hypocritical ulema and was executed in Balkh in 1711 on charges of blasphemy.

## 10. Tashkent in the 18th and early 19th centuries

### Tsarist rule

In the first half of the 18th century, Tashkent was dependent on the Dzungars and Kazakhs; In 1747, the Tashkent Khojas executed the Kazakh governor and seized power. The city was divided into four quarters—Sebzar, Shaykhantakhur, Kökcha, and Beshagach—which were governed by clan leaders and nobles. Such rule was called "black rule" (four rulers).

### Yunushoja and the Hokimiyat of Independent Tashkent (1784-1808)

In 1784, Yunushoja was elected as the sole ruler at a large assembly. He consulted with the elders of the neighborhood, reinforced the city's defensive walls, and developed agriculture. In 1788–1796, Chimkent, Sayram, and Turkestan were annexed. In 1799, the Kokand army was defeated at Karasu on the banks of the Chirchiq River, and Kurama was annexed. Relations with Russia were established: in 1786, Tashkent merchants visited the Irbit fair; In 1803, the embassy of Tashkent was received by Alexander I. In 1803, Yunushoja died; his son Sultanhoja surrendered to the Khanate of Kokand in 1808, and the period of independent rule ended.

### City appearance

At the end of the 18th century, the area of Tashkent was 16 km2, and the population was 80-100 thousand people. The height of the defensive wall was 5-10 meters, and the number of gates was initially 8 and then 12 (Kashgar, Labzak, Beshagach, Kamalon, Samarkand, Kökcha, Chigatay, Sagban, Karasaray, Takhtapul, Kokand, Kuymas). Water supply depended on the Kaykovus, Anhor, Labzak, and other canals. The mahallas specialized (Degrez — blacksmiths, Sedelnik, Jeweler); the city had 16 caravanserais and 26 specialized market areas (200 shops in the grocery market).

## 11. The Liquidation of the Khanate and Its Historical Significance

### Russian invasion and the liquidation of the khanate

The seizure of Tashkent in 1865 sharply worsened the political and economic situation of the Kokand Khanate. The khanate lost its most important trade centers and military fortifications on the banks of the Syr Darya, and its foreign trade became dependent on the conditions established by Russia. Trying to maintain trade and diplomatic relations with Russia, Khudoyar Khan chose a policy of compromise with the Russians. However, this policy intensified dissatisfaction among the population, especially among the nomadic Kyrgyz and Kipchak clans. The burden of taxes and duties, as well as additional payments collected for the palace's needs, worsened the lives of the masses.

In 1875–1876, a widespread popular uprising broke out against the power of Khudayar Khan, and the khan was forced to seek protection from Russia. Under the pretext of restoring order in the conditions of the uprising, Russian troops entered the territory of the khanate, and in early 1876, the Khanate of Kokand was liquidated. The Fergana region was established on its territory and became part of the Turkestan Governor-Generalship. Thus ended the nearly century-and-a-half reign of the Ming dynasty.

### Historical significance

The Kokand Khanate is a state that played an important role in the history of Central Asia, and its historical significance is manifested in several aspects. Firstly, the khanate contributed to the political unification of the Fergana Valley and the strengthening of economic and cultural ties between the valley's population within a single state. Secondly, together with Bukhara and Khiva, it preserved the traditions of independent statehood in the region until the mid-19th century. Thirdly, the khanate experienced a period of significant economic growth due to the construction of large canals, the growth of cities, and the development of specialized crafts.

In the cultural sphere, Kokand became a major center of Uzbek literature and historiography under the patronage of Umar Khan and Nadirabegim. Poets such as Nadirabegim, Uvaysi, Gulkhani, Makhmur, and others made a great contribution to the development of Uzbek literature, while the Kokand school of architecture and calligraphy were distinguished by their unique style. At the same time, the crisis of the khanate provides a historical lesson: the contradictions between the sedentary and nomadic population, the weakness of the central government, the struggle for the throne, and the pressure of external forces show that even the strongest state can lead to decline.

## Conclusion

The Khanate of Kokand was founded in the early 18th century under the leadership of the Ming clan leader Shah Rukhbi amidst the weakening of the Ashtarkhanid state and the threat of the Dzungars. Abdulkarimbi moved the capital to Kokand and defended the independence of the khanate; Under Narbutabi, Alim Khan, and Umar Khan, central power strengthened, and the territory expanded to Tashkent, Turkestan, and the banks of the Syr Darya. In the mid-19th century, the confrontation between the sedentary and nomadic population, the struggle for the throne, and conflicts with Bukhara led to the crisis of the khanate, which was taken advantage of by the Russian Empire, which liquidated the khanate in 1876. Nevertheless, the Kokand architectural school, historiography, and literary environment formed by artists such as Nodirabegim, Uvaysi, and Gulkhani are bright pages of Uzbek culture.

## Key concepts

- The Ming dynasty was a Kokand dynasty of rulers from the Ming dynasty (1709–1876).
- The legend of the Golden Cradle is a legend invented to justify Ming power.
- Mingboshi is a high military-administrative position introduced by Umar Khan.
- The bekdom was the main administrative unit of the khanate.
- **Amlak ("own land")** — lands belonging to the khan.
- Tanho, accent, mulki hur are forms of land ownership.
- Tanabona is a tax collected from each tanab of orchards and vegetable gardens.
- The Kokand literary environment is a creative circle formed under the patronage of Umar Khan and Nodirabegim.
- **Four khokimiyats** — the management of Tashkent by the heads of four quarters.
