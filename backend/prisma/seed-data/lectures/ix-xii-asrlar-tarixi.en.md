## 1. Collapse of the Arab Caliphate. Tahirid

### The situation in the caliphate

By the 9th century, in the outskirts of the caliphate, the desire for independence among local dynasties intensified: the Umayyads in Spain, the Idrisids in Morocco, and the Tulunids in Egypt and Syria began to conduct themselves practically independently. After the death of Caliph Harun al-Rashid in Tus in 809, a struggle for the throne began between his sons al-Amin (Baghdad) and al-Ma'mun (Marv). The aristocracy of Khorasan and Maveraunnahr supported al-Ma'mun. The Caliphate was forced to involve local nobles in governance: the Barmakids of Balkh achieved great positions in central power, but in 803, Harun al-Rashid ordered their extermination. These events highlight the internal weakness of the caliphate and the growing influence of the local elite.

### Tahirids (821-873)

In the service of Tahir ibn al-Husayn al-Ma'mun, the ruler of Bushang near Herat, he defeated al-Amin's army at Ray in 811; for cutting the sword with both hands, he received the nickname "Zul-Yaminayn." In 813, he captured Baghdad. In 821, when he was appointed ruler of Khorasan, he stopped pronouncing the caliph's name in Friday sermons-this was a sign of independence. In 822, he died suddenly.

The water distribution laws "Kitab al-Kuni," compiled by the order of Abdullah ibn Tahir, served as the basis for resolving water disputes in Maveraunnahr for hundreds of years. Abdullah issued a decree prohibiting the oppression of peasants: "Allah feeds us with their hands..." In Tahir's will to his son, it is stated that the treasury increases only when it is spent on the needs of the people.

## 2. Saffarids

In the 9th century, gazis-voluntary armed groups consisting mainly of ruined peasants and artisans-were created to protect against nomads. The coppersmith (saffar) was the leader of the brothers Yaqub and Amr ibn Leys, who captured the center of Seistan, Zaranj, in 861 and Nishapur in 873, ending the reign of the Tahirids. In 876, Yaqub's campaign against Baghdad ended in failure. The people did not support the Saffarids due to increased taxes and religious separatism; In 900, Amr ibn Layth was defeated by the Samanids.

## 3. The Samanid state

### Sources

The main sources of the Samanid period are the works of Narshahi "Tarihi Bukhara," at-Tabari "Tarih ar-Rusul wa-l-muluk," Gardizi "Zayn ul-Akhbar" and the works of Arab geographers Istahri, Ibn Havkala, Muqaddasi.

### The flourishing of the dynasty

The dynasty was founded by the descendants of Samankhudat, the elder of the village of Saman near Balkh. In 819, by order of al-Ma'mun, Asad's sons were appointed governors of the provinces: Nuh in Samarkand, Ahmad in Fergana, Yahya in Shash and Ustrushana, and Ilyas in Herat. In 874, at the request of the Bukhara nobility, Nasr sent his younger brother Ismail ibn Ahmad as the ruler of Bukhara; In 875, Nasr received a charter from the caliph granting him the right to govern all of Transoxiana. In 888, Ismail won a clash with his brother; After Nasr's death in 892, he became the rightful ruler of Maveraunnahr and moved the capital from Samarkand to Bukhara.

### Ismail Samani (892-907)

In 893, Ismail captured Taraz and Ustrushana and put an end to the nomadic invasion. In the spring of 900, he defeated the army of Amr ibn Lays at Balkh; he armed the Bukharan artisans and the common people in battle. In the first battle, he surprised the prisoners by freeing them. By uniting Maveraunnahr, Khorasan, and the eastern regions of Iran, he put an end to the dominance of the Arab Caliphate. Narshahi quotes his words: "As long as I am alive, I will be the fortress of Bukhara."

### Crisis and completion

During the reign of Nasr II (914-943), the state was ruled by the viziers Jayhani and Bal'ami; the movement of the Karmates led to unrest. During the reign of Nuh ibn Nasr (943-954), in 942, kharaj was collected twice, and soldiers were not paid a salary. Having grown stronger, the commander of the Turkic guard, Alptegin, captured Ghazni in 961. During the reign of Noah II (976-997), the Karakhanid Harun Bughra Khan captured Bukhara in 992; The ruler of Khorasan, Abu Ali Simjuri, and the commander Faiq changed. On October 23, 999, Bukhara was captured by Nasr Elekhan, and Abdulmalik II was sent to the prison of Uzgand. The last Samanid, Ismail al-Muntasir, was killed in 1005. The decline of the Samanids was the result of internal conflicts, the arbitrariness of the military elite, and the financial crisis.

## 4. State administration and economy during the Samanid period

### Dargahs and Divans

The supreme ruler held the title of emir. The administration was divided into a dargah (emir's palace) and a divan. Narshahi writes that during the reign of Nasr II, separate buildings for 10 divans were built in the Registan of Bukhara.

| Divan | Function |
|---|---|
| Cabinet of Ministers | The Prime Minister's office controlled all the offices |
| Divan Mustafī | Financial affairs |
| Divan Amid ul-Mulk | Official documents and diplomatic correspondence |
| Divan-sahib ash-shurat | Military affairs, four salaries a year for the army |
| Divan Sahib al-Barid | Mail and secret surveillance (centralized only) |
| Divan mukhtasiba | Monitoring compliance with market, weight, and Sharia rules |
| Devoni Mamlakati Khos | Palace expenses |
| Divan of waqf | Waqf property |
| Qadi's Divan | Judicial affairs |
| State Property Office | Dynastic possessions |

Provinces were governed by khokims, and cities by rais, darugha, and shina. The head of the clergy was called "Ustod," and later "Sheikhulislam." Bukhara became one of the most prestigious centers of the Islamic world, where the first madrasas in Maveraunnahr were built.

### Land ownership

The main forms of land ownership were: the sultan's property-the lands belonging to the emir, mills, and shops; private property-the lands of nobles, sayids, and merchants, which took the forms of iqta (hereditary) and tuma (lifelong); waqf-the property of religious institutions; and communal property-pastures and rainfed lands.

### Household

Agriculture was based on artificial irrigation: the Shapurkan, Shahrud, Dargom, and Ishtikhan canals were used; cotton, wheat, rice, and grapes were grown, and dried fruits were sent to Europe. In the textile industry, the fabrics of Bukhara Zandan and Samarkand Vedar ("Khorasan Flakes") were known in Iraq, Iran, and India. In pottery, enameled vessels appeared, and glassmaking and mining developed. Samarkand paper (six varieties) was known before Europe. In trade, Samarkand was the main market of Maveraunnahr: horses and glassware were exported to China, and silk was imported; Dried fruits, fabrics, and silver were exported to Khazar, Bulgarian, and Russian lands, and furs and leather were brought in.

## 5. The Karakhanid State

### Formation

In the middle of the 10th century, Turkic tribes-the Karluk, Chigil, and Yagma-established their own state in Semirechye and Kashgar. Abdulkarim Satuk Buğra Khan, having captured Taraz and Koshgar, took Balasagun in 927 and declared himself supreme ruler; he was the first of the Karakhanids to convert to Islam. Under his son Moses, in 960, 200 thousand Turkic residents converted to Islam. The word "Karakhan" means "black" — "strong," "great," "khan" — "ruler."

### Conquest of Maveraunnahr

In 992, Harun Bughra Khan captured Samarkand and Bukhara, but returned due to illness and died in 993. In 999, Nasr Elekhan captured Bukhara and put an end to the Samanid state. In 1001, by agreement with Mahmud of Ghazni, the border was determined along the Amu Darya. In 1041, the state was divided into Western (Movarounnahr, capital Bukhara, then Samarkand) and Eastern (Zhetirdaryo, Kashgar, capital Balasagun, then Kashgar) parts.

### Ibrahim Tamgach Khan (1040-1068)

Sources describe Ibrahim Tamgach Khan as a strong and just ruler: he fought robbers, regulated trade and monetary circulation, built a hospital and a madrasah in Samarkand in 1066, and put an end to the Karmat movement.

### Dependence and Termination

In 1089, the Seljuk Melikshah captured Samarkand and Bukhara. From 1102, the Karakhanids became effectively dependent on Sultan Sanjar; After the Battle of Katwan in 1141, he began paying tribute to the Karakits. From 1210, it was dependent on the Khwarazmshahs; After the uprising in Samarkand in 1212, the last khan, Osman Khan, was executed, and the dynasty was liquidated.

### State structure and culture

Power passed not from father to son, but from brother to brother, then to the next generation; the entire clan was considered the collective owner of power. The supreme ruler was the Great Khagan (Tamgach Khan), and the regions were governed by elekhans. The two capitals are Kashgar and Balasagun. The palace officials were the Grand Hajib, the Bitikchi (Munshi), the Yalavach (Ambassador), and the Kushchi; the army was called a cherik, and the commander was a subashi. The common people were called Sart, and merchants were called Sart. The iqta form of land ownership became widespread.

During the Karakhanid period, Turkicization intensified, and literature in the Turkic language emerged ("Kutadgu Bilig," "Devonu Lug'otit Turk"). The Kalyan Minaret (1127) was built in Bukhara, the Jarkurgan and Vobkent minarets, and the Raboti Malik mausoleum and caravanserai in Uzgen.

## 6. Ghaznavids

### Formation

The Samanid commander of the Turkic guard, Alptegin, captured Ghazni in 961. The former slave he raised, Sabuktegin (977-997) of the Barshan tribe, became effectively independent while ruling Ghazni as the Samanid governor.

### Mahmud Ghaznavi (998-1030)

In 999, Mahmud separated Khurasan from the Samanids; the caliph bestowed upon him the title "Yamin ud-dawla wa amin ul-milla." Between 1002 and 1026, he made 17 campaigns against India. In 1017, at the Battle of Hazarasp, he defeated the Khwarazmian army and took Beruni and other scholars to Ghazni. In 1029, he conquered Rai and Jibal and became the owner of the largest state in the Muslim East. He waged a ruthless struggle against the Qarmatians and Ismailis. In Ghazni, he built the first state madrasah (1019), a cathedral mosque, and a dam; Unsuri, Farruhi, Utbi, Gardizi, and Bayhaqi created works in the palace.

The textbook emphasizes that in Soviet historiography, Mahmud Ghaznavi is presented only as a "robber"; in reality, he was a contradictory but great commander and a major statesman.

### Crisis

His son Masud (1030-1041) was defeated by the Seljuks in the Battle of Dandanakan in May 1040 and lost Khorasan. Eastern Afghanistan and part of Northern India remained in the hands of the Ghaznavids; In 1186, the Gurids put an end to the dynasty. The administration consisted of a dargah and five divans (vizier, military, official documents, finance, post); the Grand Hajib was called the most influential official, the commander-in-chief of the army was called the sipahsalor, and the head of the region was called the vali.

## 7. Seljuks

### Origin

The Seljuks were originally from the Qiniq clan of the Oghuz and came to Jand during the reign of their leader, Seljuk ibn Dokok, and converted to Islam. In 1025, Mahmud of Ghazni allowed 4,000 Seljuk families to cross the Amu Darya and settle in the vicinity of Saraxs, Farova, and Obivard.

### Nizam-ul-Mulk

Abu Ali ibn Ishaq of Tus was the chief minister of Alp Arslan and Malikshah for nearly 30 years (1063-1092); the sultan gave him the title "Nizamulmulk" ("State Charter"). He wrote the work "Siyosatnoma" and opened "Nizomiya" madrasas in cities to train officials. He was killed by an Ismaili spy in 1092. Amir Temur gave a high assessment of his activities in his "Documents."

### Management

The supreme ruler was Sultan ul-A'zam. The residence was served by the Grand Hajib, Amir Haras, and Salahdar; there were official divans-Devon Tugra (orders), Divan Istif (finance), Divan Ishraf (state control), and Divan Arz (military affairs). The influence of military commanders grew particularly strong after the defeat of Katvan.

## 8. The State of the Khwarazmshahs and Anushtagins

### Khwarazmshah dynasties

The title of Khwarazmshah was held by various dynasties: the Afrigians (305-995, capital Kat), the Ma'munids (995-1017, capital Gurganj; Ma'mun Academy), descendants of the Ghaznavid governor Altyntas (1017-1034) and the Anushtagins (1097-1231). It was under the Anushtagins that this title reached its peak.

### The flourishing of the dynasty

Anushtagin was from the Begdili Oghuz clan and was sold into slavery in his youth; He was a Tashdar at the court of Malikshah and received the title of Khwarazmian Shinkh. His son Qutbiddin Muhammad (1097-1127) received the title of "Khwarazmshah" in 1097; The enlightened ruler, who received his education in Marw, served Sultan Sanjar faithfully. Atsiz (1127-1156) began a struggle for independence: he captured Jand and Mangyshlak; In 1138, he was defeated by Sanjar, but in 1139, he regained power; the caliph recognized him as sultan; From 1141, he minted gold coins in his own name. In the textbook, Otsiz is considered the true founder of the Khwarazmshah-Anushtagini state.

Al-Arslan (1156-1172) began to refer to himself in letters to the Seljuks not as a "gang," but as a "mukhlis" (friend); In 1167, he captured Nishapur. Takish (1172-1200) won a 20-year struggle against his brother Sultan Shah; In 1187, he took Nishapur; In 1194, he defeated the last Sultan of the Iraqi Seljuks, Toghrul III, and completely destroyed the Seljuks; Relations with Baghdad Caliph al-Nasir were severed. Muhammad Khwarazmshah (1200-1220) defeated the Gurids; In September 1210, he defeated the Karakits in the Ilamish steppe and conquered all of Maveraunnahr; In 1212, he suppressed the uprising in Samarkand and put an end to the Karakhanid dynasty.

## 9. Science and culture in the 9th-12th centuries

### Centers of Science and Education

The 9th–12th centuries in the history of Maveraunnahr and Khurasan, along with political fragmentation, are characterized by a high level of scientific and cultural development. Local dynasties-the Samanids, Karakhanids, Ghaznavids, Seljuks, and Khwarazmshahs-tried to gather scholars, poets, and historians in their palaces, as patronage of science was considered an important means of increasing the ruler's authority. Bukhara, Samarkand, Marv, Nishapur, Urgench (Gurganj), and Balkh became major scientific centers of the Islamic world. Madrasas, libraries, and scientific collections operated in these cities.

### Great scientists

During this period, representatives of world-class medieval science emerged. Muhammad al-Khwarizmi (IX century) is known as the founder of algebra, whose works were translated into Latin in Europe and influenced the development of mathematics and astronomy. The hadith collection compiled by Imam al-Bukhari (810-870) is recognized as one of the most reliable sources in the Islamic world. Abu Nasr al-Farabi (873-950) created many works in the fields of philosophy, logic, music, and natural sciences, earning the title of "the second teacher in the East after Aristotle." Abu Rayhan Biruni (973-1048) is the author of major works on astronomy, geodesy, geography, history, and mineralogy. Abu Ali ibn Sina (980-1037) influenced Eastern and European medicine for many centuries with his major works on medicine and philosophy, especially "The Canon of Medicine."

The works of Yusuf Hos Hajib's "Kutadgu Bilig" and Mahmud Koshgari's "Devonu Lug'otit Turk" hold a special place in the culture of Turkic peoples. The first is the first major philosophical and moral dastan written in the Turkic language, and the second is a dictionary and encyclopedic source of Turkic languages. In historiography, Narshaxi's work "Tarikh-i Bukhara" is a valuable source describing the medieval history of Bukhara. In the 11th-12th centuries, Mahmud al-Zamahshari created major works in the field of linguistics, Burhaniddin al-Marginani in the field of fiqh, and Ahmad Yasawi wrote hikmahs in the Turkic language, making a significant contribution to the development of Sufi literature.

### Architecture and Applied Arts

In architecture, the Samanid mausoleum in Bukhara (IX-early X centuries), Minaret Kalyan (1127), Minaret Jarkurgan, the Raboti Malik caravanserai, and other monuments demonstrate the architectural achievements of the era. The collection of bricks, ornamental decoration, and the construction of domed structures reached their peak during this period. In applied arts, glazed ceramics, glassmaking, artistic metalworking, and textiles developed, while Samarkand paper contributed to the widespread dissemination of knowledge and culture.

### Historical significance of the era

The 9th-12th centuries became an important stage in the history of Uzbek statehood. During this period, local dynasties freed themselves from the power of the Arab Caliphate and restored their independent political life, forming complex systems of state administration (devans, iqta, waqf). At the same time, a unified regional culture emerged as a result of the interaction between Turkic and Iranian cultural traditions. In later periods, including the Timurid Renaissance, they relied on the scientific and cultural heritage of this period. Therefore, the 9th–12th centuries are often called the period of the Eastern Renaissance.

## Conclusion

In the 9th-12th centuries, the weakening of the Arab Caliphate led to the strengthening of local dynasties in Mawarannahr and Khorasan. The Tahirid dynasty took the first step toward independence, while the Samanid dynasty, led by Ismail Samani, completely ended the power of the caliphate and created a centralized state and a ten-divan system of government. Turkification and literature in the Turkic language developed during the Karakhanid period; The Ghaznavids and Seljuks created vast empires in the Middle East, and Nizam al-Mulk's "Siyasat-name" made a great contribution to the theory of statehood. In the 12th century, the Khwarazmshah-Anushtaginids under Atsiz, Takish, and Muhammad became the most powerful state in the Muslim East. This period holds an important place in history as a period of revival of Uzbek statehood traditions and a high cultural upsurge.

## Key concepts

- **Khalifat** — Arab-Muslim state; It began to disintegrate in the 9th century.
- **Gazi** — voluntary armed groups fighting against nomads.
- **Dargokh and Divan** — palace and state institutions (ministries).
- **Sahib al-Barid** — postal and secret control.
- **Mukhtasib** — controller of the market and Sharia rules.
- **Iqta** — land provided in exchange for services or the right to collect taxes from it.
- **Ilek Khan, Tamgach Khan** — Karakhanid titles.
- **Ismaelis, Karmates** — separatist religious movements.
- Khwarazmshah is the ancient title of the ruler of Khwarazm.
